# CHAPTER FIVE — 23ANDME: THE PRECISION-MEDICINE MIRAGE


There was a moment, sometime in the late 2000s, when it felt as if Silicon Valley had discovered genetics. Glossy ads began appearing—people spitting into tubes, smiling at the promise of unlocking their “true selves.” It was a seduction: precision medicine without the hospital gown, genomics without the biopsy, a revolution that could be shipped to your doorstep in a cardboard box.

At first glance, 23andMe looked like the opposite of everything that had gone wrong in Henrietta Lacks’s time. Their branding announced empowerment. “Take control of your health.” “Discover what makes you, you.” “Your data, your decisions.” It was genomics democratized—consumerized, even—freed from the sterility of medical centers and placed directly into the hands of the public.

But the closer I looked, the more familiar it seemed.

The same architecture.
The same asymmetries.
The same economic logic.

Just packaged with better typography.

23andMe promised precision medicine to the masses, but what they actually delivered was ancestry—because ancestry was the only thing their data was strong enough to support. The health panels they launched with were based on effect sizes too small to offer meaningful clinical guidance. They could tell you where your great-grandparents came from, but not what drug you should take for your depression, or whether your breast cancer risk was actionable.

And yet, the company was valued at billions.

Why?
Because the true product was not the report.
It was you.

23andMe sold the illusion of personal insight. But what made them lucrative to investors had nothing to do with your ancestry breakdown or your carrier status. It was the dataset—millions of genotypes, deeply phenotyped, reconsented for commercial use through Terms of Service written by lawyers instead of ethicists.

In 2018, GSK invested $300 million into 23andMe for a single reason:
to use the dataset to accelerate clinical trial recruitment and drug discovery.¹

That investment revealed what 23andMe really was:
a precision-medicine brokerage.
A middleman between your DNA and Big Pharma.

The ancestry reports were the loss leader.
The health panels were the marketing.
The genomic database was the asset.

This was not a precision-medicine company.
It was a precision-marketing company with biomedical window dressing.

And once again, the dividends flowed upward.

⸻

The Illusion of Representation

23andMe claimed it was empowering individuals to understand their health risks. Yet its early datasets shared the same foundational flaw as CHOP’s, as the EGG consortium’s, as nearly every early genomic initiative in the world: they were overwhelmingly of European ancestry.

In fact, according to a landmark NEJM review (Popejoy & Fullerton, 2016),
over 80% of all GWAS participants globally were of European descent,
while less than 2% were of African ancestry.²

23andMe’s entire analytic pipeline rested on models derived from these skewed cohorts. Their risk predictions were strongest for the people already most represented in the scientific record, and weakest for the communities that carried the highest burdens of disease.

This was not a 23andMe-specific failure. It was the structural reality of the field. But the company packaged these biases in a consumer-friendly interface, turning systemic inequity into a lifestyle product.

You could “learn your risk” only if your genome resembled the genomes that had built the risk models. Everyone else received noise.

And so once again, the narrative of empowerment masked the reality of exclusion.

⸻

The Dollar Value of a Human Being

23andMe’s business model was built on a profound asymmetry: they collected DNA for $99 per kit, but the commercial value of each individual in their database was exponentially higher.

A 2018 analysis by investment banks estimated that each fully phenotyped, reconsented genomic participant was worth approximately $1,000,000 in downstream R&D and trial-recruitment value to a major pharmaceutical partner.³

This is not a metaphor.
This is not an exaggeration.
This is the math of modern drug development.

Pharma companies spend billions recruiting patients for trials.
23andMe solved that bottleneck by building a direct-to-patient pipeline.
A genomic marketplace.
A precision-medicine dragnet.

For the average person, the value proposition was an ancestry breakdown and a handful of low-impact health insights.
For the company, each participant was an annuity.

This was the precision-medicine economy in its purest form:
a single human genome as a seven-figure asset on the balance sheets of someone else’s future.

Not far removed from Henrietta Lacks.
Not far removed from the men of Tuskegee.
Not far removed from the communities in West Philadelphia whose biology powered genomic discovery without ever participating in its gains.

The technology changed.
The economics did not.

⸻

The Promise They Couldn’t Keep

23andMe promised that individuals could “take control” of their health. But precision medicine doesn’t live in the consumer domain. It lives in the clinical one—where risk scores must be validated against diverse populations, where effect sizes must be meaningful, where decisions carry medical consequences.

But 23andMe did not have the diversity required to make those claims real.
Their BRCA reports, for instance, covered only the three founder mutations common in Ashkenazi Jewish populations.⁴ For everyone else, the models were underpowered and incomplete.

When the FDA cracked down in 2013, it wasn’t because 23andMe was malicious. It was because the science wasn’t there. The panels could not reliably predict risk. The ancestry breakdowns did not translate into actionable clinical decisions. The company had built a health narrative on genomic sand.

And yet, even as the FDA halted their health reports, the database continued to grow.

Even when stripped of clinical authority, the company’s value increased.

Because the pipeline—the genomic-to-pharma funnel—did not depend on whether the public-facing reports were accurate.

It depended on whether the database was big.

⸻

The Invisible Bargain

23andMe presented itself as a consumer revolution, but the truth is that participants were not the customers—they were the product.

This is the same invisible bargain that underpins the modern AI ecosystem.
Data in. Value up. Dividends upward only.
Patients and consumers left holding the illusion of empowerment while their contributions fuel trillion-dollar cycles of innovation.

When I saw the GSK partnership announced, the whole architecture snapped into focus.
This wasn’t democratization.
This was centralization.
Another reconfiguration of the same lineage that began with HeLa:
biology transformed into economic capital, with no pathway for value to flow back down.

Once again, the story was not about villains.
23andMe did not invent inequity.
They scaled within it.

Just like CHOP studied the population it could reach,
23andMe recruited the population that already had the privilege, means, and interest to purchase a genomic test.

The structure, not the company, wrote the narrative.

But the effect was the same:
those most at risk of disease contributed the least to the genomic record,
and those least affected shaped the models we began to call “precision.”

⸻

Why This Matters for the Next Economy

By the time 23andMe went public through a SPAC in 2021, the gap between the public story and the economic reality had widened into a chasm.

The story was about insight.
The reality was about extraction.

The story was about empowerment.
The reality was about asymmetry.

The story was about precision medicine.
The reality was that no dividends returned to the millions of people whose DNA built the company.

And here is the uncomfortable truth:
23andMe is not an outlier.
It is a prototype.

Its logic governs biobanks.
Its logic governs real-world evidence.
Its logic governs AI training sets.
Its logic governs EHR mining.
Its logic governs the digital exhaust collected from every medical encounter.

This is the architecture we inherited.
But it does not have to be the architecture we keep.

A new economy is possible—one where provenance, consent, agency, and compensation circulate alongside scientific discovery. A world where the next GSK partnership, the next HeLa, the next COVID dataset does not simply reproduce the inequities of the past.

This chapter marks the turning point.
We have traced the dividends.
We have traced the deaths.
We have traced the lineage of extraction.

The next chapters trace how to rewrite the system.

Because if Henrietta Lacks revealed the first stolen dividend,
and 23andMe revealed the modern mechanism of extraction,
then the only question left is:

What would it take to build an economy where patients finally receive what they are owed?

The answer begins with who is missing.
And why.
