## Overview

Modern medicine runs on an economy most people never see.

Every lab test, tumor sequence, scan, risk score, and clinical encounter is translated into a code—the billable code—that allows human life to be rendered legible to institutions. These representations move farther, faster, and with more authority than the people they describe. Decisions are made on the abstraction first, and only later—if ever—on the body itself.

This is not a glitch. It is the operating logic of the precision medicine economy.

Patients generate the biological and clinical data that make this system possible, but they do not control how those representations are used, transferred, or monetized. The economic benefits flow upward—to pharmaceutical companies, genomic testing firms, hospital systems, AI vendors, and data brokers—while accountability dissipates. What remains is a system optimized for scale without custody, value without provenance, and representation without responsibility.

In older traditions, mistaking representation for reality had a name. It was not called illusion because it was false, but because it was incomplete. In this book, that phenomenon appears as Digital Māyā: the institutional shadow of a person that systems act on first.

This book argues that when Digital Māyā scales without constraint, imbalance becomes structural—and lethal.

Modern medicine operates as geometry: people are embedded into representational spaces; care is delivered through boundary decisions; and outcomes follow from where a person is placed in that space. Systems act first on representation and only later—if ever—on the body.

Those actions are executed by three canonical engines of the precision medicine economy:
- **Discovery** (who is seen and studied)
- **Care** (who is treated, delayed, or denied)
- **Information** (which representations persist, travel, and compound)

When failures in these engines cannot be corrected, mortality becomes the system’s only remaining audit.

The precision medicine economy is therefore paid in two currencies: dividends and deaths.

Dividends accrue to those whose genomes, tumors, and clinical profiles are well represented in datasets, trials, and models. Deaths fall disproportionately on those excluded from discovery—patients underrepresented in trials, invisible in algorithmic training sets, or misclassified by systems that were never built to see them. Breast cancer makes this asymmetry impossible to ignore. Black women in the United States experience approximately 40% higher mortality despite similar incidence. Globally, survival collapses in low-income countries. Precision medicine performs best for the patients who need it least, and worst for those who need it most.

This is not biological fate. It is representational imbalance.

Deaths in this system are not accidental. They are the predictable output of misgoverned engines operating on flawed geometry, biased by incentives and constraints, and left uncorrected by law or custody.

The story begins with Henrietta Lacks, whose HeLa cells powered modern molecular biology and generated incalculable downstream value without consent, custody, or equity. It continues through consumer genomics companies that marketed empowerment while quietly constructing clinical-trial recruitment pipelines worth millions per participant. It extends to the present moment, where a circular AI economy mirrors—and accelerates—the same extractive logic medicine spent decades trying to regulate.

Healthcare eventually recognized that ungoverned extraction causes harm. Through HIPAA, HITECH, the 21st Century Cures Act, and the Information Blocking Rule, the United States built the strongest data-rights framework in the world. These laws did not eliminate representation. They imposed order on it—rules for access, consent, correction, and accountability. These laws function as mortality governance: they determine whether errors can be corrected before harm becomes fatal.

AI has no equivalent constraint.

In death-bound terms, AI does not introduce new risks—it removes the remaining friction between representational error and patient harm.

What is missing is not innovation, data, or models. What is missing is governance: enforceable alignment between representation and consequence, value and responsibility. Older civilizations had a word for this kind of order. They called it Ma’at.

Ma’at is not moral aspiration in this book. It is the governance condition required once failure can kill.

Dividends & Deaths exposes the hidden mechanics of the precision medicine economy and argues that restoring balance requires rendering Ma’at operational—through provenance, custody reassignment, and patient-owned value. It introduces the concept of the medical dividend, not as charity or incentive, but as structural correction. And it insists on a single measure of success: whether mortality gaps shrink.

If they do not, the system has failed—no matter how precise it claims to be.

## Canonical Systems Guidance

This book treats death as a systems outcome, not a tragedy of chance.

Every preventable death is traced to failure in at least one canonical engine—Discovery, Care, or Information—operating on flawed geometry and left uncorrected by absent or unenforced governance.

The reader is never asked to accept outrage without mechanism. Every claim of harm is paired with structure, and every structural failure is paired with consequence.
