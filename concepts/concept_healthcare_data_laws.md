# Healthcare Data Laws

## Purpose
Summarize the U.S. policy decisions that shaped how health data is collected, controlled, and monetized.

## Scope
Applies to chapters that discuss data rights, provenance, or governance.

## Canonical Definition
Healthcare data laws are the U.S. policy decisions that determine how health data is collected, controlled, and monetized. In this book, they function as mortality governance: the rules that decide whether failures in Discovery, Care, and Information can be corrected before harm becomes fatal.

### Direct-to-Consumer Drug Advertising: An American Anomaly
The United States (along with New Zealand) is virtually alone in allowing prescription drug makers to market directly to the public. The result is demand shaped by media, not clinical context: patients ask for branded drugs, physicians face pressure, and marketing becomes a demand‑side engine. This is the first visible conversion of attention into claims—an early example of representation outrunning custody.

### Massive Subsidies to Digitize Health Records
The 2009 HITECH Act subsidized the nationwide rollout of electronic health records (EHRs), updating HIPAA for the digital era and paying providers to adopt certified systems. The policy succeeded in scale—digitization went near‑universal—but it also hardened the logic of legibility: care had to be rendered in fields, codes, and compliance artifacts to move money. This is Digital Māyā at scale: representation becomes the operating surface.

### HIPAA: Securing Data at the Expense of Sharing
HIPAA (1996) created the privacy and security foundation for health data and formalized patient access rights. Its unintended consequence was a culture of lock‑down: institutions interpreted privacy as non‑movement, and interoperability stalled. The system gained protection but lost flow—custody without circulation, a partial form of Ma’at.

### The Patient Portal Paradox (Meaningful Use and Its Discontents)
Meaningful Use required patient portals, but most portals functioned as read‑only windows into siloed systems. Patients could view, not move; see, not correct; access, not control. This is access without custody—Digital Māyā with no key. The 21st Century Cures Act and its Information Blocking Rule (finalized in 2020) shifted policy toward portability via standardized APIs (FHIR). Enforcement began in 2023, reframing data as something that must travel with the patient. The arc is toward Ma’at: enforceable alignment between access and agency.

### AI and the New Data Economy in Healthcare
AI sits downstream of digitization. The same pipelines that standardized records now feed models, and the incentives tilt toward high‑value codes rather than low‑visibility patients. This is Māyā compounding: representations multiply, custody lags. If governance does not catch up, AI becomes the most efficient extractor yet.

In death-bound terms, AI does not introduce new risks. It removes the remaining friction that once slowed the translation of representational error into patient harm.

### From Disease-Centric to Patient-Centric Care
Across these policies, the pattern is stable: the system rewards what it can code. The corrective move is not sentiment—it is structure: custody, provenance, correction, and enforceable access. That is Ma’at rendered operational.

## Relationship to Canonical Concepts
- **Three Engines (Discovery / Care / Information):** healthcare data laws determine where and when engine failures can be corrected before death occurs.
- **Geometry of Medicine:** these laws decide whether positions in representational space are auditable, portable, and correctable.
- **Digital Māyā:** data laws either constrain or permit representations to govern action without custody.
- **Ma’at:** healthcare data law is Ma’at rendered operational—enforceable alignment between representation, power, and consequence.

When these laws fail or are absent, mortality becomes the system’s only remaining audit.

## Death-Bound Clarification
In this book, healthcare data law is treated as **mortality governance**, not privacy policy.

A data law is relevant only insofar as it can:
- Prevent patient death by enabling correction, appeal, or intervention
- Or permit patient death by allowing errors to persist without accountability

Laws matter not because they regulate data, but because they determine whether failures in **Discovery, Care, and Information** can be corrected before harm becomes fatal.
