# Healthcare Data Laws

## Purpose
Summarize the U.S. policy decisions that shaped how health data is collected, controlled, and monetized.

## Scope
Applies to chapters that discuss data rights, provenance, or governance.

Use as background framing; cite specific laws when making policy claims. Keep the tone structural and aligned with Digital Maya (representation without custody) and Ma’at (enforceable alignment).

## Direct-to-Consumer Drug Advertising: An American Anomaly
The United States (along with New Zealand) is virtually alone in allowing prescription drug makers to market directly to the public. The result is demand shaped by media, not clinical context: patients ask for branded drugs, physicians face pressure, and marketing becomes a demand‑side engine. This is the first visible conversion of attention into claims—an early example of representation outrunning custody.

## Massive Subsidies to Digitize Health Records
The 2009 HITECH Act subsidized the nationwide rollout of electronic health records (EHRs), updating HIPAA for the digital era and paying providers to adopt certified systems. The policy succeeded in scale—digitization went near‑universal—but it also hardened the logic of legibility: care had to be rendered in fields, codes, and compliance artifacts to move money. This is Digital Maya at scale: representation becomes the operating surface.

## HIPAA: Securing Data at the Expense of Sharing
HIPAA (1996) created the privacy and security foundation for health data and formalized patient access rights. Its unintended consequence was a culture of lock‑down: institutions interpreted privacy as non‑movement, and interoperability stalled. The system gained protection but lost flow—custody without circulation, a partial form of Ma’at.

## The Patient Portal Paradox (Meaningful Use and Its Discontents)
Meaningful Use required patient portals, but most portals functioned as read‑only windows into siloed systems. Patients could view, not move; see, not correct; access, not control. This is access without custody—Digital Maya with no key. The 21st Century Cures Act and its Information Blocking Rule (finalized in 2020) shifted policy toward portability via standardized APIs (FHIR). Enforcement began in 2023, reframing data as something that must travel with the patient. The arc is toward Ma’at: enforceable alignment between access and agency.

## AI and the New Data Economy in Healthcare
AI sits downstream of digitization. The same pipelines that standardized records now feed models, and the incentives tilt toward high‑value codes rather than low‑visibility patients. This is Maya compounding: representations multiply, custody lags. If governance does not catch up, AI becomes the most efficient extractor yet.

## From Disease-Centric to Patient-Centric Care
Across these policies, the pattern is stable: the system rewards what it can code. The corrective move is not sentiment—it is structure: custody, provenance, correction, and enforceable access. That is Ma’at rendered operational.

## Relationship to Digital Maya and Ma’at
- **Digital Maya:** These laws show how representation became the operating surface and why custody lagged.
- **Ma’at:** These laws supply the enforceable alignment tools (access, correction, provenance, non‑blocking) that make governance real.
