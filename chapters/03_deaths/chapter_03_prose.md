## **Deaths: Where the Burden Falls**

> **Chapter Question:** Where do deaths accumulate when representation fails?  
> **Chapter Claim:** Mortality follows representation; when data is incomplete and models ignore comorbidity, deaths concentrate in the communities the system was never built to see.  
> **Exit Handoff:** If deaths are the downstream ledger, we must trace how extraction began and why it was normalized.

When I began working in genomics and clinical data, I thought I understood inequity. I had seen the skewed datasets, the missing codes, the fragmented care pathways that erased entire communities from the narrative of precision medicine. I knew the system was imbalanced. I did not yet grasp that mortality is where every imbalance cashes out.

Mortality is the downstream ledger. It has no spin. It is where incomplete representation becomes a body count. You can argue about algorithms, about data quality, about the elegance of a model. You cannot argue with survival curves.

Mortality is the final code. It is the last entry in the record, the last billable event in a life. The system can disagree about a diagnosis, a lab, a score. It cannot disagree that a life ended. That is why mortality is the truest measure of how the system treats the represented and the unrepresented.

The system measures itself in efficiency, speed, and innovation. It rewards throughput, novelty, and the creation of new codes, new biomarkers, new revenue lines. But mortality is what remains after the accounting is done. It is what happens when representation fails to keep pace with the life it is meant to describe.

That is why this chapter sits where it does. It is the turning point in the arc. It is where the economy’s arithmetic becomes visible in the body. It is where the distance between code and person becomes a difference in survival.

Breast cancer is where this inequity becomes impossible to ignore. Black women are roughly 40% more likely to die of breast cancer than White women, despite lower incidence [@desantis2022_breast_cancer_stats]. This statistic has been reported in NEJM, JAMA Oncology, and The Lancet [@daly2022_breast_cancer_disparities]. No serious dataset contradicts it. There is no dispute in the literature.

And yet, breast cancer is one of the most studied diseases in human history. It is not an orphan condition. It is not rare. It is not technologically neglected. It is not underfunded.

If precision medicine were working as intended, this should be the disease where inequities shrink. Instead, they widen.

The reason is not a mystery. The pathway from screening to survival is long and fragile. It depends on early detection, follow-up imaging, pathology, staging, referrals, therapy, adherence. Each step is mediated by codes, coverage, and system capacity. Missing a step does not mean missing a number. It means missing a chance.

This is not biology. This is architecture. The billable code is the fundamental economic unit of American healthcare. Codes are not neutral. The Billable Code makes care legible; when that legibility thins out, deaths accumulate. The code determines which histories are visible, which symptoms are reimbursable, which follow-ups are triggered. It is the operating grammar of clinical reality.

That grammar does not treat everyone equally. It does not have to. It merely has to be consistent enough to render people billable. The system can function while the person beneath the code is fragmented, delayed, or misclassified. In an economy built on representation, the fidelity of the representation becomes the difference between life and death.

The moment I understood this most clearly was not in a lab or a dataset. It was in the quiet spaces between appointments where patients ask if the machine sees them. It was in the gap between a diagnosis and a follow-up, between a biopsy and the therapy that never came. That gap is not mysterious. It is structural. It is where representation gets dropped and mortality begins to rise.

We do not experience inequity only at the point of diagnosis. We experience it across the entire chain of evidence. Each missing code narrows the options for the next clinician. Each missing appointment creates a reason to delay. Each delay reshapes survival. The code does not intend to be cruel; it does not have to. It only has to be incomplete.

The precision-medicine economy is built to translate biology into manageable units. It does not translate evenly. The translation is cleaner for those with stable access, continuous coverage, and records that persist across institutions. For everyone else, the translation degrades. In those gaps, the system cannot do what it claims it can do. The mortality curve rises to meet the absence. This is extraction in slow motion: value captured upstream while risk is distributed downstream.

The system is a ledger, and ledgers are ruthless. If you are not in the ledger, you are not in the equation. If you are not in the equation, you are not in the model. That is the quiet path by which invisibility becomes mortality.

Even partial representation can mislead. A thin record can read as low risk when it is actually a sign of poor access. The system treats that thinness as reassurance. Care waits longer. What looks like safety in the code often hides danger in the body.

Follow a patient from first symptom to final code and the mechanism becomes clear. A lump is noticed at home. A clinic visit is scheduled weeks out because work and childcare are nonnegotiable. The intake note captures pain but not concern. A screening order is placed, but the referral is routed to a center outside the patient’s network. Imaging is delayed. A biopsy is scheduled, then rescheduled. A diagnosis is made and coded, but the oncology visit arrives late. A recurrence score requires a complete record, so it is never ordered. A therapy that requires a specific staging code is never authorized because the staging code was never entered. Every step is a reasonable decision inside the system. Each one is a small loss of representation. The losses accumulate, and the body bears the consequence.

This is what the Billable Code does when it becomes the only grammar in the room. It compresses a life into billable units and leaves everything else outside the record. The things that are hardest to code are often the things that matter most: the delays, the missed appointments, the unstable housing, the work schedule that turns care into a series of deferrals. The code is not designed to carry those realities, so the algorithm never learns them, and the system never accounts for them.

It also shifts incentives. Clinicians are required to document what is reimbursable. Systems are optimized around what can be billed. The result is a record that tilts toward the measurable and away from the lived. When the record becomes a financial artifact, the person becomes a financial abstraction. That is how representation drifts away from reality without anyone deciding it should.

## **The UCSF Revelation — Diseases Don’t Come One at a Time**

At UCSF, I worked down the hall from Esteban Gonzalez Burchard, one of the clearest voices in genomic inequity. His work made something plain: minority patients do not experience disease one condition at a time, and yet precision medicine—built on reductive models—pretends they do.

Burchard’s research showed:
	•	Black and Latino children suffer the highest asthma mortality rates in the country [@akinbami2012_asthma_disparities]
	•	Puerto Rican children have the highest asthma prevalence in the world, nearly 3× that of White children [@nishimura2015_latino_asthma]
	•	African ancestry is associated with worse asthma exacerbations, even when controlling for environment [@kumar2015_african_asthma]
	•	The genetic variants that drive asthma risk in minorities are often absent in Eurocentric GWAS (genome-wide association study) datasets, making clinical prediction tools less accurate for the people who need them most [@popejoy2016_diversity_failure]

These were not modest findings. They were indictments.

What struck me was not just the data, but the contradiction. The same systems that claimed to be precise were forced to generalize across populations they had never actually measured. They sounded confident because they were blind to the inputs they were missing.

Burchard also demonstrated something even more fundamental: most minority patients experience comorbidities that Eurocentric precision medicine never models together. A clinic can treat asthma; a dataset can isolate asthma; a model can predict asthma. A patient lives with asthma and obesity and anxiety and hypertension and diabetes and environmental exposure all at the same time.

Asthma and obesity.
Asthma and preterm birth.
Asthma and exposure to pollution concentrated in redlined neighborhoods.
Asthma and stress from systemic racism.
Asthma and hypertension.
Asthma and diabetes.

Eurocentric precision medicine saw one disease. Patients lived with five.

The model failure is not accidental. It is built into the architecture. The Billable Code breaks the body into discrete, billable units. That is efficient for reimbursement and terrible for biology. The more comorbidities you have, the more fragments your story becomes. A fragmented story is easy to encode but hard to care for.

In the chart, these fragments become separate problems with separate codes, separated visits, separated billing rules. The system is optimized for singular encounters, not for plural lives. That is why a patient with multiple conditions often becomes “complex” in administrative language. Complexity is simply another word for “hard to code.”

Complexity also becomes a reason to exclude. In clinical research, comorbidities are treated as confounders. In algorithm development, they are treated as noise. Clean datasets are prized. Complex datasets are seen as messy. But for the patient who carries multiple risks, the “mess” is the reality. The model’s cleanliness is paid for by the patient’s invisibility.

Data cleaning is never neutral. Outliers get removed. Missing values get imputed. Cases with partial records get dropped. Each of these steps makes the model more elegant, and each makes the model less faithful to the lived world. When you remove the hardest cases to simplify a dataset, you teach the model that those cases do not exist. When those cases then show up in the clinic, the model fails silently. The failure looks like a low risk score. The patient receives a late diagnosis.

In meetings where cohorts are selected, you can hear the tradeoffs in plain language: we need a clean cohort, we need a manageable dataset, we need to control for variables. These are technical decisions. But they produce moral consequences. Every exclusion criterion is a decision about whose life counts as evidence.

And if your story is encoded across multiple disconnected systems, it becomes invisible to the very models that claim to see you. These models were trained to recognize the patterns most common in the data. They do not understand missingness as signal. They treat missingness as absence. In communities where data is scattered, missingness becomes destiny.

The human consequence is not just poor prediction. It is misdirected care, delayed care, or care that never arrives.

## **The Multiplicative Nature of Inequity**

When you place these conditions on top of each other, something startling emerges. For many minority patients, risk is not additive—it is multiplicative.

Asthma × pollution × poor access to specialty care × housing instability × hypertension × diabetes × lack of genomic testing = an entirely different disease trajectory.

Yet the models used to predict risk—polygenic scores, machine-learning prognostics, EHR-based algorithms (built on electronic health records)—were built on the assumption of a single disease in a single body.

These models are only as good as their denominators. The denominator is the dataset the model sees. If the dataset excludes complexity, the model learns simplicity. If the dataset excludes the hardest cases, the model learns to expect easy ones. It is not the algorithm’s fault. It is the dataset’s shadow.

This is why the same algorithm can look brilliant in a paper and brittle in a clinic. In a controlled dataset, the variables are clean. In a real clinic, variables are incomplete. In a community clinic, variables are missing. The model doesn’t fail because it is wrong. It fails because the world is more complicated than the representation it was trained on.

Multiplicative risk is not just a metaphor. When exposures stack, each one reshapes the impact of the others. A model trained on isolated conditions cannot capture these interactions. It treats each factor as a separate line item. But biology does not behave like a ledger. The human consequence is a risk score that underestimates the true danger.

There is a second failure that often goes unacknowledged: missingness is not random. The people with the most fragmented data are usually the people with the most fragmented access. When a model treats missing data as neutral, it is not neutral. It is a proxy for structural exclusion. The algorithm sees a blank entry and interprets it as normal. The patient is treated as lower priority.

Risk is also dynamic. A model often sees a snapshot: a list of codes, a handful of labs, a single visit. But lives are not snapshots. Risk rises and falls across seasons of stress, across changes in coverage, across the churn of housing and work. When the record captures only fragments, the model treats stability as the default. In communities where stability is the exception, the model systematically underestimates the danger.

And the absence of a code is often misread as health. A missing diagnosis can mean a missing test, a missing visit, or a missing clinic. But the algorithm sees a blank entry as a normal state. The system then treats the patient as lower risk and allocates resources elsewhere. The absence of representation becomes a rationale for further absence.

The same pattern holds across conditions:

Hypertension

Black Americans develop hypertension earlier and with greater severity. Yet the foundational genetic risk models come almost exclusively from European ancestry biobanks [@hoffmann2018_gwas_bias].

When you build a risk model on one ancestry and apply it to another, you are not doing precision medicine. You are doing projection.

Diabetes

Latino, Native, and Black patients have significantly higher rates of diabetes and earlier complications. Yet the clinical prediction algorithms (HbA1c thresholds, a blood sugar measure used for diabetes risk scoring) are least accurate for them [@osei2021_diabetes_disparities].

If the threshold is wrong, the alarm comes late. The human consequence is a complication, not a correction.

Pregnancy & Preterm Birth

Black women face a 50% higher risk of preterm birth, even after controlling for income, insurance, and education [@taylor2020_preterm_birth].

The human consequence is a newborn whose first days are spent in a NICU because the model never treated the mother as high risk.

Kidney Disease

APOL1 variants dramatically increase chronic kidney disease (CKD) risk for African ancestry patients—variants that were unknown for decades simply because no one sequenced enough African ancestry genomes [@genovese2010_apol1_ckd].

The human consequence is a delayed diagnosis for a risk that was always there.

COVID-19

Black Americans died at twice the rate of White Americans early in the pandemic.
Latino mortality climbed 4–5× in several states.
Native communities suffered devastation unmatched in modern U.S. epidemiology.
[@hill2021_covid_inequities]

These are not separate problems. They are the same problem: a healthcare system built on data from populations least affected by disease. The models do not fail because the math is wrong. The models fail because the datasets are incomplete.

When the system does not see you, it cannot model you. When it cannot model you, it cannot predict your trajectory. When it cannot predict your trajectory, it cannot prevent your death.

As Burchard put it in one of his most cited papers:

“Precision medicine for the few is not precision medicine.”
—Esteban Gonzalez Burchard, NEJM, 2016 [@popejoy2016_diversity_failure]

The phrase could serve as the epitaph of the first generation of precision medicine.

## **The Geography of Absence**

When I began doing breast cancer work in Florida, it became obvious how deeply these inequities ran. Breast cancer mortality was just the surface layer.

Underneath were layers of comorbidity—hypertension, diabetes, delayed screening, inconsistent imaging, environmental exposures, and the coded architecture of clinics that served as gatekeepers to precision therapies. The same patient could be seen by a primary care physician, a community clinic, and a hospital system, but their data might never reconcile. Each visit generated a billable trace. The person underneath the trace remained fragmented.

The system does not just care about whether you were seen. It cares about whether you were seen in the right place, by the right specialist, inside the right network, and with the right code. Those constraints are not clinical. They are economic. They are the walls of the maze patients must navigate to be counted.

Women with dense coded histories were eligible for genomic recurrence scoring. Women with fragmented histories were not. This is how the Billable Code becomes a sorting mechanism. It determines who is legible enough to receive the next level of care.

Women who received timely follow-up after an abnormal screening mammogram lived significantly longer [@fedewa2020_mammography_delay]. Women who waited months—often due to transportation, work schedules, insurance churn—died more often.

The mortality curve was not determined by tumor biology. It was determined by data completeness.

This is the institutional mirror. It reflects a representation of the patient and then acts on that reflection first. The more complete your data, the longer you live. The less complete your data, the higher your risk of dying from a disease we already know how to treat.

This is why precision medicine “works” on paper: the people with the best access to healthcare also have the most data. The people with the most data also get the most precision. And therefore the best outcomes. The science looks impressive because the pipeline selects its own winners.

That is not a flaw in the system. That is the system.

In Central Florida, that logic takes physical form. A patient can live within miles of advanced oncology centers and still be functionally excluded by appointment lag, transportation, or the simple reality that the specialist network does not include them. The representation of their care never enters the algorithm. The algorithm therefore never learns them. The clinic never sees the risk it cannot compute. The cycle continues.

The geography of absence is not only a map of where clinics are. It is a map of where data travels and where it stops. It is a map of which codes cross institutional boundaries and which do not. The person may cross those boundaries, but their record often does not.

The human consequence is a disease treated too late and a death that looks inevitable only because the system did not recognize it in time.

Data completeness is a privilege. It arrives with stable employment, with insurance that persists, with time to attend appointments, with transportation that does not fail. It is not just a technical condition. It is a social one. When the system selects for data completeness, it selects for those privileges. Everyone else becomes an outlier not because their biology is rare, but because their representation is incomplete.

The absence is compounded by interoperability gaps. Records do not travel cleanly across institutions, and they travel even less cleanly across economic boundaries. Community clinics are often the first stop for care and the last stop for data. The person moves through the system; the record stays behind. That is how a patient can be present at every appointment and still be invisible to the model.

This is where the institutional mirror becomes dangerous. The system acts on the representation because it is scalable. It cannot act on the person because the person is complex. The representation becomes the target of care. When the representation is incomplete, the care is incomplete, and the consequence is mortality. The ledger is balanced only in the abstract. The body pays the difference.

Custody is the missing word in that sentence. If the representation is the thing the system acts on, then whoever controls that representation controls the distribution of care. When custody is held by institutions whose incentives are aligned with throughput, representation will drift toward what is profitable, not what is just. Mortality exposes that drift. It is the only audit that does not accept excuses.

## **Clinical Trials — Where Future Deaths Are Decided**

Precision oncology trials determine who will receive the next generation of therapy. They are the mechanism by which the future becomes real.

And they are massively inequitable.

A JAMA Network Open analysis shows that:
	•	Black patients are 40–60% underrepresented
	•	Hispanic patients are 50% underrepresented
	•	Asian and Indigenous patients are drastically underrepresented

These disparities exist even in trials for diseases that disproportionately affect minority groups.

[@loree2021_trials_underrepresentation]

A NEJM review found that:

81% of all genome-wide association study participants are of European ancestry.
African ancestry participants represent <2%.
Latino and Indigenous ancestry combined: <1%.

[@popejoy2016_diversity_failure]

This is not lack of effort. It is lack of economic incentive. Recruitment is easiest where the system already functions. Everywhere else becomes expensive. When the system measures cost and speed above all else, representation becomes a luxury.

The mechanics of exclusion are quiet. Trials favor patients with fewer comorbidities because they are easier to analyze. They favor patients who can take time off work to show up. They favor patients whose primary language matches the consent forms. They favor patients already seen inside the academic networks where trials are run.

Eligibility criteria are often designed for clarity, not reality. They remove the “noise” of comorbidity. But in doing so, they remove the patients who carry that noise. The dataset becomes clean, the paper becomes publishable, and the resulting evidence becomes less applicable to the people who need it most.

When a trial excludes complexity, it excludes reality. The evidence it produces is clean, but the care it informs is incomplete. This is how the future of therapy is decided by those least affected by disease.

The human consequence is a treatment that arrives late or not at all.

And the consequences do not stop at the trial’s edge. Trial results become guidelines. Guidelines become reimbursement decisions. Reimbursement decisions become what is possible inside a clinic. If the evidence is built on people who were easy to recruit, the resulting standard of care will fit those same people best. This is how underrepresentation compounds: first you are excluded from evidence, then the evidence is used to exclude you from care.

Trial geography reinforces this loop. Trials are anchored in the places where research infrastructure already exists, which means academic centers and well-resourced systems. The patients most likely to be recruited are those already inside those networks. Community clinics that serve the most underrepresented patients are often outside the research economy. They do not have coordinators, protected time, or the administrative capacity to run trials. The result is predictable: the trial becomes a mirror of the system’s reach, not of the disease’s burden.

Consent itself is a form of representation. It assumes trust, clarity, and time. But trust is not evenly distributed. It is shaped by history, by prior harm, by the feeling that the system will protect or exploit. When trust is low, participation drops. The dataset narrows. The evidence becomes less applicable. The exclusion is not malicious. It is structural.

I learned that lesson viscerally during my decentralized COVID-19 imaging trial at the University of Central Florida (UCF).

## **COVID-19 — The Exposing Event**

When the pandemic shut down clinical research, the inequities I had spent decades studying erupted in plain sight. Social determinants of health—usually relegated to grant introductions—became front-page news.

Black Americans died at nearly twice the rate of White Americans in early phases of the pandemic. Latino mortality rates reached catastrophic highs in some states. Native communities saw mortality ratios higher than anything in modern American epidemiology [@hill2021_covid_inequities].

In the early months, access itself was the determinant. Access to testing. Access to care. Access to a ventilator in a system where everything was suddenly scarce. The communities most likely to work in public-facing jobs were the ones most exposed, least protected, and least resourced. The data mirrored the exposure.

The pandemic made the data gap visible in a new way. It showed that the people who were least represented in data were also the people most likely to be harmed by the lack of data. It was a cruel feedback loop: the system did not see them, and therefore it did not serve them, and therefore it continued not to see them.

The shift to remote care amplified this loop. Communication moved to portals. Instructions moved to automated messages. Follow-ups became links, uploads, and forms. These pathways assume bandwidth, devices, and digital fluency. When those are unevenly distributed, so is the data. The pandemic dataset becomes biased toward the people who could stay connected. That bias then becomes a source of future policy, future models, and future preparedness plans. The missing are missing twice.

I quickly shifted my research and launched one of the earliest decentralized imaging-data trials in the country—NCT05384912, registered at ClinicalTrials.gov [@hadley2022_covid_imaging_trial]. Patients could upload chest imaging from home or hospital portals. The trial informed my book chapter on COVID-19 and the emerging metaverse of medical data.

But the most telling discovery came from our recruitment experiment.

We used Facebook ads to recruit participants. The cost difference was staggering:
•	Under $200 to recruit a White woman [@hadley2022_covid_imaging_trial]
•	Over $9,000 to recruit a Black woman [@hadley2022_covid_imaging_trial]

A 45× cost differential for the same digital outreach [@hadley2022_covid_imaging_trial].

This was inequity in its purest form—no biology, no clinics, no radiologists, no insurance companies. Just a platform and an algorithm revealing the price of reaching people the system had long ignored.

The lesson was not only about marketing costs. It was about who the digital economy considers reachable, desirable, and profitable. The same logic that prices ad audiences also prices patients, and the price determines who is recruited into evidence and who is left to live outside it.

The pandemic showed how devastating inequity could be, and it revealed something else.

The COVID-19 vaccine trials—for all their flaws—were among the most equitable trials in American history. Not because the system suddenly learned equity, but because the entire country needed the vaccine. Everyone had to be included or the economy could not reopen.

It was the rare moment where survival demanded inclusivity.

Once the crisis passed, inequity returned—this time wearing digital clothing.

The digital clothing matters because it looks neutral. It looks like convenience. It looks like access. But it often assumes devices, bandwidth, and digital fluency that are unevenly distributed. A system that moves faster than its least connected patients does not move toward equity. It moves away from it.

The platform economy adds another layer of asymmetry. The datasets that look massive are often massive only for the people already connected. The missing are not missing by accident; they are missing because the infrastructure never reached them. When those datasets become the substrate for new models and new products, the exclusion becomes a feature, not a bug. The system scales its blind spots.

## **The Arithmetic of Death**

The precision-medicine economy measures success by innovation: new drugs, new biomarkers, new AI models. But death measures something else: the distribution of that innovation.

Death accumulates where:
	•	comorbidities cluster
	•	environments degrade health
	•	data disappears
	•	codes go missing
	•	follow-up lapses
	•	trust erodes
	•	recruitment fails
	•	economics block access
	•	history repeats itself

Death accumulates exactly where the system has not been designed to see.

This is where the institutional mirror becomes dangerous. The system acts on the representation because it is scalable. It cannot act on the person because the person is complex. The representation becomes the target of care. When the representation is incomplete, the care is incomplete, and the consequence is mortality. The ledger is balanced only in the abstract. The body pays the difference.

Custody is the missing word in that sentence. If the representation is the thing the system acts on, then whoever controls that representation controls the distribution of care. When custody is held by institutions whose incentives are aligned with throughput, representation will drift toward what is profitable, not what is just. Mortality exposes that drift. It is the only audit that does not accept excuses.

When I look at the mortality curves today—in breast cancer, in asthma, in heart disease, in diabetes, in COVID—I see the reflection of two decades of datasets I analyzed, engineered, or inherited.

I see my patients. I see the women of West Philadelphia. I see the families of San Francisco’s Mission District. I see the neighborhoods of Central Florida.

And I see my daughters—standing at the intersection of every underrepresented ancestry, every structural barrier, every algorithmic blind spot, every coded omission.

The precision-medicine economy was never abstract to me after that. It was personal.

Mortality is the only KPI that matters. If the mortality gap does not shrink, then no amount of innovation, no number of AI models, and no volume of published papers can be called success. The system is accountable to the bodies it represents, not to the metrics it celebrates.

That is the point of this chapter. It is not a catalog of tragedies. It is a diagnosis of a representational economy that confuses the abstraction for the person. The death count is the system’s audit. The rest of this book is about how to pass it.

This chapter is built to make the mechanism legible, not just the grief. It shows that dividends and deaths are not separate outcomes; they are the same pipeline viewed from two ends. The system rewards legibility and penalizes absence. That is the inversion at the heart of the precision-medicine economy. The chapters ahead will trace how that inversion was built and how custody can be reassigned so that representation stops being a privilege and becomes a right.

The rest of this book is about what we must do—structurally, economically, and morally—to ensure the next generation of precision medicine does not replicate the inequities of the last. To understand how extraction began and why it was normalized, we have to return to its origin story: Henrietta Lacks.

Dividends rise. Deaths fall.
