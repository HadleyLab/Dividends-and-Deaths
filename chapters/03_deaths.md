# Chapter Three — Deaths: Where the Burden Falls

> **Chapter Question:** If the dividends of precision medicine rise through coded systems, where do the deaths accumulate when those systems fail to see us?  
> **Chapter Claim:** Mortality follows representation, not biology. Every gap in the datasets that power precision medicine reappears as a gap on the survival curves.  
> **Exit Handoff:** We have traced the flow of value; the next sections expose the mechanisms of extraction that keep the majority unseen.

---

## The Ledger No One Can Ignore

When I began designing genomic pipelines, I thought inequity was an upstream data problem—missing codes, uneven recruitment, fractured ontologies. Only later did I realize that mortality is the downstream proof that those upstream absences are lethal. You can argue about access, participation, or workflow; you cannot argue with a death certificate.

Breast cancer makes that ledger visible. Black women in the United States remain roughly **40% more likely to die** of breast cancer than White women despite slightly lower incidence. The number appears in *CA Cancer Journal for Clinicians*, *NEJM*, *JAMA Oncology*, *The Lancet*. It has been replicated across registries and time periods. There is no counter-dataset waiting to correct it.

This is not biology. This is architecture.

Precision tools—molecular subtyping, recurrence scoring, imaging AI—perform best for the people most accurately represented in the data. Everyone else lives with miscalibrated models, delayed screening, fractured follow-up, and clinical trials that rarely call their names.

---

## Diseases Never Travel Alone

At UCSF, Esteban González Burchard’s work made one thing incontestable: minority patients do not experience disease one diagnosis at a time. Asthma, obesity, pollution exposure, hypertension, diabetes, preterm birth, structural racism—these interact multiplicatively. Eurocentric precision medicine, however, analyzes them as isolated variables because its datasets were never calibrated for multiplexed burdens.

Examples repeat across conditions:

- **Asthma:** Puerto Rican children have the highest prevalence in the world; African American children die at rates up to 10× higher. Yet the GWAS data used to build risk scores is largely European.
- **Kidney disease:** APOL1 variants—common in West African ancestry—dramatically increase CKD risk. They went undiscovered until 2010 because African genomes were absent from discovery pipelines.
- **Pregnancy:** Black women face a 50% higher risk of preterm birth even after controlling for insurance, income, and education. Models built on homogenous cohorts cannot anticipate those trajectories.
- **Hypertension and Diabetes:** Earlier onset, more severe complications, less accurate prediction tools for the populations with the highest burden.

Every time a population is underrepresented upstream, the deaths stack downstream.

## West Philadelphia, Again

When I was training at CHOP, I stood on the hospital roof one night and looked out over West Philadelphia—the same neighborhoods my family drove through on Sabbaths when I was a boy, the same streets wrapped around the precision engines I was helping build. Inside the hospital we were coding genomes with exquisite precision. Outside, women with abnormal mammograms waited months for follow-up because transportation, childcare, or employment made “timely” a privilege. When those delays occur, mortality climbs. *Cancer* journal quantified it: a woman who waits more than 60 days after an abnormal mammogram is significantly more likely to die. Delay is not an inconvenience; it is a survival determinant.

Those women live five miles from one of the best hospitals in the world, and yet the system records them as absences—as missed screenings, uncoded biopsies, unsequenced tumors. The dividends travel through the rooftop antennas. The deaths remain on the stoops below.

## The Personal Break Point

I did not fully absorb the stakes until I returned home each night, long after my daughters were asleep, and realized that the systems I was architecting had no place for them. They are Trinidadian, carrying Indian, African, Venezuelan, and European lineages braided through generations of colonial migration. When I searched the major reference datasets—the Human Genome Project, early GWAS catalogs, large-scale sequencing panels—I could not find a single category that described them accurately.

In the eyes of the system, my daughters are statistical ghosts. Their biology does not fit the reference, so the models cannot account for their risks. The same inheritance that was celebrated in our Prologue—mangoes and Malta and mauby, Adventist Sabbaths and Hindu weddings—renders them invisible inside the coded abstraction that now determines who lives and who dies.

That realization was not sentimental. It was operational. When my daughters enter their own care trajectories, every algorithm built on miscalibrated data will underserve them. The mortality curve is not an abstract graph; it is a call sheet for my family.

## COVID-19: Inequity in Real Time

The pandemic forced America to watch inequity unfold in real time. Black mortality doubled White mortality in early phases. Latino mortality spiked 4–5× in several states. Native communities experienced devastation unmatched in modern epidemiology. Suddenly, the social determinants of health we tucked into grant introductions became the nightly news.

During that period I launched a decentralized imaging study (NCT05384912), believing that digital recruitment might flatten the old barriers. The opposite happened. Facebook advertising revealed the cost of reaching people the system had ignored:

- **< $200** to recruit a White woman to upload imaging.
- **> $9,000** to recruit a Black woman.

Forty-five times more expensive to reach the population with the highest mortality. No ultrasound suites. No waiting rooms. Just an algorithm measuring who the healthcare economy has historically seen. Digital Maya in action: the system acts on the abstraction it has, not the lives it should serve.

---

## The Arithmetic of Death

Every innovation press release touts dividends—new biomarkers, new AI, new targeted therapies. Mortality counts the opposing column. Death accumulates where:

- Comorbidities cluster without corresponding data.
- Environments degrade health faster than clinical models assume.
- Codes go missing, making follow-up invisible.
- Recruitment budgets deem certain lives “too expensive.”
- Trust erodes because history has given every reason for skepticism.

The underrepresented majority—Black, Brown, Indigenous, immigrant, diasporic, mixed-ancestry communities—generate the least usable data and suffer the most lethal outcomes. They are not “minorities.” They are the majority of humanity, rendered minor because the system sees them less.

---

## The Pivot to Reconstruction

Part I ends here because the next sections cannot simply describe inequity; they must dismantle the architecture that makes it inevitable. We cannot continue to translate human reality into codes that omit most of humanity and then feign surprise when deaths gather in predictable places. The Prologue showed you the first stolen dividend. Chapter One taught you to see the code. Chapter Two followed the money. Chapter Three is the reckoning: until representation changes, precision is theater.

From here, the book pivots. The question is no longer “Who dies?” We already know. The question is “Who controls the abstraction that decides?” That is where extraction becomes explicit, where Henrietta’s legacy meets 23andMe’s machinery, and where the blueprint for a different economy finally comes into view.
