# CHAPTER FOUR — HENRIETTA LACKS AND THE IMMORTAL DIVIDEND

The first time I encountered the name Henrietta Lacks, it was not in a lab. It was in a training module — one of those early rites of passage every aspiring scientist must complete before touching a pipette or opening a clinical dataset. In my case, it was CITI certification: slides about human subjects protections, informed consent, and the legacy of research abuses that made those protections necessary. Henrietta’s name sat beside the Tuskegee syphilis study as if the two were bookends — different transgressions, same architecture. Lives transformed into data. Data transformed into discovery. Discovery transformed into profit. And nothing ever returned to the people at the center of the story.

Her story was presented as a warning, as a historical wound the system had tried to heal. But when I clicked through those slides — HIPAA modules, IRB recertifications, the annual reminders every researcher endures — Henrietta felt more like an icon than a person. A symbol of what used to happen.

What I did not know then was that her story was not past tense.
It was present tense.
It was everywhere in the system I was joining.

Years later, at Penn, I would see HeLa cells stored in freezers like ordinary lab tools. Every major hospital, every major research institution keeps them. They are as foundational to biomedical science as the microscope. By the time I began working on computational genomics and the earliest models that evolved into NeuroFix Therapeutics, those cells had already become atmospheric — part of the background of science itself. We did not use HeLa cells in our validation work; we used immortalized neuronal cell lines appropriate for studying neurodevelopment. Ethical, consented, commercially available. The kind of tools any lab in the world would use.

And yet.

Those cell lines — that entire infrastructure of immortalized human biology — existed because of the precedent HeLa set. Because one woman’s cells grew when others did not. Because the scientific world took what her body produced and built an empire on top of it.

I did not understand this fully at Penn.
Or at CHOP.
Or at Stanford.
Or even at UCSF.
Not at first.

I had been taught Henrietta’s story as morality.
It took years before I saw it as economics.

The Woman Behind the Cells

Henrietta Lacks was a 31-year-old Black mother of five in 1951 when she entered Johns Hopkins Hospital with pelvic pain. Hopkins was the only major hospital in Baltimore that treated Black patients during segregation, and the colored ward was understaffed and overburdened. A biopsy of her cervical tumor was taken as part of her clinical care. She did not know another portion was sent to the laboratory of George Gey.

What happened next changed the course of biomedicine.

Her cells did not die.

They divided faster than any human cell previously cultured. They were resilient, robust, astonishingly adaptable. Gey recognized the significance instantly. He began sharing the cells freely with researchers nationwide. From there, HeLa cells multiplied into every corner of modern science.

They helped develop the polio vaccine.¹
They made HPV vaccine development possible.²
They enabled virology breakthroughs, cancer drug testing, radiation studies, gene-mapping, IVF, and early HIV research.³
More than 75,000 scientific papers explicitly cite them.⁴
Millions more used them indirectly.

No other human cells have reshaped medicine so profoundly.

And yet, Henrietta’s children did not know her cells existed until decades later — when researchers contacted them not to offer care, but to request blood samples to help distinguish HeLa contamination in other cell lines.³

The Lacks family struggled financially, often uninsured, while their mother’s cells helped generate billions of dollars in downstream products, patents, and scientific tools. By the time Thermo Fisher Scientific settled with the family in 2023—after being accused of profiting from HeLa without consent for years—the economic harm was already incalculable.⁵ Legal filings against Novartis and Viatris followed in 2024.⁶

No economic analysis can quantify the value extracted from HeLa.
But one truth is undeniable:
the first dividend of the precision medicine economy was stolen.

⸻

Echoes Across My Career

The more I traveled through the architecture of genomics and clinical medicine, the more often Henrietta’s story resurfaced—not as a single tragedy, but as a pattern.

At CHOP, during my MD/PhD training, I worked with one of the largest pediatric genomic datasets in the world: more than 30,000 children genotyped through the Center for Applied Genomics (CAG). It was an extraordinary scientific endeavor. CHOP and CAG did not exclude anyone; they studied the children who walked through their doors. They did not design the insurance system. They did not design the geography of West Philadelphia. They did not design who had the means, the transportation, the time, or the generational trust required to participate in research.

They studied the population they had access to.
They did not define the population.

And yet, the demographic reality was impossible to ignore:
more than 99% of the children in that dataset were White.

This was not CHOP’s failure.
It was America’s.

A country where residential segregation, insurance patterns, and socioeconomic barriers determined who reached specialty clinics — and therefore whose DNA entered the genomic record. CAG’s science was rigorous, groundbreaking, and ethical. But the dataset it produced was shaped upstream by structural inequity.

It was, in a sense, a digital HeLa economy:
valuable biology flowing upward, invisibility flowing downward.

When CHOP’s ambitions outgrew its own clinical catchment, it joined the EGG Consortium, an international effort dominated by European ancestry cohorts. Again, this was not malice — it was scale. Europe had the infrastructure, the biobanks, the sequencing centers. But the result was the same: genomic discovery engines powered by homogeneity, accidentally sidelining the populations who carried the highest burdens of asthma, ADHD, metabolic disease, and environmental stress.

At Stanford, in pathology and laboratory medicine, I saw the coding architecture that formalized this pattern. LOINC, SNOMED, ICD, CPT — the UMLS system that allowed data to flow smoothly through hospital billing pipelines. Codes gave medicine its structure, but they also gave it a way to abstract people from their biology. A way to convert lived experience into billable units. A way to standardize inequity.

At UCSF, working on the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), BD2K, STARGEO, and CrADLe, I entered the digital realm where Henrietta’s legacy was everywhere and nowhere. Tumor samples, blood samples, genomic sequences — all de-identified, all freely reusable, all infinitely valuable. None of them traceable back to the people who contributed them. None of them part of a revenue-sharing or benefit-sharing structure. None of them tied to a medical dividend.

It was HeLa again, scaled up to millions.

And then COVID arrived.

During my decentralized imaging-data trial at UCF (NCT05384912), patients could upload chest images from home. It should have been democratizing. Instead, the recruitment data revealed an old truth dressed in new clothing: it cost less than $200 to enroll a White woman, and more than $9,000 to enroll a Black woman. That is a 45× disparity — in digital space, with no hospitals, no transportation barriers, no schedule conflicts.

The inequity was not biological.
It was algorithmic.
It was structural.
It was economic.

And it was the same architecture that once allowed a Black woman’s cells to be taken from her body without permission and turned into the most valuable biological material in human history.

⸻

The Lineage of Exploitation

As a physician–scientist, I often reflect on how Henrietta’s story was taught to me. It was presented alongside the Tuskegee syphilis study — another chapter where Black bodies were used to advance medicine without their informed consent and at catastrophic human cost. Both cases now live in IRB modules, ethics exams, HIPAA trainings — as if repeating their names were enough to prevent repetition of the pattern.

But science is not shaped by stories.
It is shaped by incentives.
And incentives rarely reward restraint.

Henrietta’s legacy is not merely a moral caution.
It is an economic blueprint.

Her cells revealed what was possible when biology could be industrialized.
The world took note.
And the system grew around that possibility.

Today, data from millions of patients — including those who never consented to reuse — power AI models, biobanks, pharmaceutical pipelines, and precision-medicine startups. Their medical histories, imaging archives, genomic sequences, and digital exhaust generate enormous value.

And yet, the system still struggles to return even a fraction of that value to the people who make it possible.

Even now, Henrietta’s story lives on in every dataset where the contributors are unseen.
In every clinical trial that struggles to recruit Black and Brown patients.
In every genomic database built on homogeneity.
In every AI model trained on incomplete or biased data.
In every community whose health outcomes worsen while their data fuels the future.

Her story is not over.
It has simply changed form.

⸻

Toward a Medical Dividend

Henrietta Lacks reminds us that value can be generated from a single body and flow for generations without ever returning to the family who bore the cost. But her story also points toward another possibility — one that guided me toward the OPTS–EGO Ledger and the idea of a rebalanced precision economy.

What if patients could see where their data traveled?
What if they could track its use, its impact, its derivatives?
What if they could participate in the value it created?
What if the next immortal line — or the next digital twin — belonged, first and foremost, to the people who made it?

The question is not whether the precision medicine economy should continue.
It will.
It must.
The question is whether the next HeLa — the next foundational contribution — will be honored, governed, and shared.

Henrietta Lacks never chose immortality.
But her story offers us the chance to choose justice.
