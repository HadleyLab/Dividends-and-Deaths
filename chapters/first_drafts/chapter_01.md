# CHAPTER ONE — THE BILLABLE CODE

The first time I truly grasped the economic machinery beneath American medicine, I was a young physician, still adjusting to the long nights and the quiet hum of hospital corridors after midnight. The clinical world teaches you to listen for heart murmurs and lung crackles, but in those early years, I began to hear something else — a rhythmic clicking that came not from patients, but from the billing terminals glowing at nurses’ stations. Those screens were always illuminated, even when the rest of the floor slept.

One night, after seeing a patient with a suspicious breast lump, I sat down at one of those terminals to finish my documentation. She was a 42-year-old teacher, anxious but composed, who had discovered the lump in the shower that morning. I had examined her, arranged imaging, reassured her softly, and promised we would move quickly. The clinical encounter took minutes. But as I entered the digital labyrinth of billing fields and dropdowns that followed — the CPT codes for the exam, the ICD codes for the symptoms, the modifiers for imaging, the cross-links for follow-up tests — the scope of what I was actually doing came into view.

Her story was already receding into the background.
What filled the screen instead were codes.

Codes for the visit. Codes for the evaluation. Codes for the imaging. Codes for the radiologist’s report. Codes for the biopsy I suspected she would need. Codes that determined how much the hospital would receive, how much her insurer would pay, and how soon her care could continue.

That night, I realized that the billable code was not a peripheral detail of the system. It was the system. It was the grammar through which the entire healthcare economy spoke. And it shaped not only how institutions were paid, but what they chose to measure, prioritize, and ultimately value.

If the Prologue showed how Henrietta Lacks’s cells became the first unpaid dividend of modern medicine, then this chapter begins the story of how the billable code became the universal mechanism for extracting every dividend that followed.

Because long before precision medicine promised to tailor treatments to genomes and tumors, medicine had already built a different type of precision — a financial precision applied to every moment of care. The billable code is what transforms human biology into economic signal. It is what tells an insurer that a mammogram occurred, what tells a hospital how much an infusion is worth, what tells a pharmaceutical company which disease clusters are most profitable, and what tells an AI company what patterns exist in its training data.¹

No matter how advanced the science becomes, the code remains the translation layer between the patient and the economy surrounding her.

It is, in many ways, the Rosetta Stone of modern medicine.

## The Quiet Architecture Beneath Every Encounter

Most people do not see this architecture. They imagine that clinical care begins when a doctor enters the room and ends when the doctor leaves it. But inside a hospital, the process is reversed. Care begins when the patient is recorded in the system — when the registration clerk enters the first code — and continues long after the patient has gone home.

Every diagnosis, symptom, lab test, and scan must be represented as a discrete, standardized code. In theory, this brings order to a sprawling system. In practice, it creates an economic skeleton through which all clinical activity must pass.

The breast lump my patient discovered that morning became a series of nested designations: a symptom code attached to a diagnostic code attached to an imaging code attached to a pathology code. By the time her care reached the oncology team, her biology had been fully abstracted into a string of identifiers recognizable to insurers, auditors, data brokers, and AI algorithms — everyone except the woman herself.

What struck me over time was not the complexity of this system, but its consistency. Whether a patient arrived for a routine mammogram or an aggressive metastatic cancer, the journey was always mediated through coding. The code determined what was possible. It determined what could be billed, what could be reimbursed, and therefore what could be delivered. It was an algorithm long before we used that word in clinics.

And because of that, the billable code did something else — something far more consequential: it created the foundation upon which the entire precision medicine economy was built.²–⁴ ￼

⸻

## From Billable Events to a Trillion-Dollar Ecosystem

Every major player in modern precision medicine — radiology AI, genomic sequencing, targeted therapies, pharmaceutical innovation, clinical trials, EHR vendors — depends on coded data as both input and justification. The billable code is the inventory of the healthcare economy. It is the dataset that feeds pipelines, informs corporate strategy, shapes regulatory pathways, and identifies high-value markets.²–⁵ ￼

When a pharmaceutical company studies which cancers offer the greatest return on investment for a new targeted therapy, it does not begin with tumor biology. It begins with claims data — coded records of incidence, severity, treatment patterns, and reimbursement histories.

When a genomic testing company tries to justify coverage for a new sequencing panel, it relies on prior coded outcomes to prove clinical utility.

When a radiology AI startup assembles a training dataset of hundreds of thousands of mammograms, it must rely on coded imaging archives and coded pathology concordance pairs.

When an insurer designs a machine-learning model to predict which patients are “high risk,” it does so with coded events — and as a landmark Science article showed, the model can inadvertently embed racial bias because historic coding reflects unequal access to care.¹ ￼

Every actor in the ecosystem depends on these codes because the code is the only universally accepted unit of value.

But the patient who generates the code — the one whose mammogram or biopsy is transformed into economic signal — does not receive any share of the value created from it.

The billable code is the starting point of extraction.

⸻

## The Uneven Weight of Visibility

Coding is often treated as a neutral administrative function, but it is neither neutral nor evenly applied. Wealthier health systems invest in teams of coding specialists, sophisticated EHR workflows, AI-assisted documentation, and revenue optimization tools. Underserved hospitals, particularly in rural and minority communities, often lack these resources.

The result is that the same disease is documented differently depending on who has it and where they receive care. And differences in documentation lead to differences in reimbursement, differences in access, differences in eligibility for advanced diagnostics, and differences in inclusion in research datasets.

Breast cancer exposes this fault line with painful clarity.

Decades of high-impact research — in New England Journal of Medicine, CA Cancer Journal for Clinicians, and related ACS reports — arrive at the same conclusion: Black women are less likely to receive timely biopsies, less likely to receive HER2 testing, less likely to be offered genomic recurrence scoring, and less likely to be enrolled in precision-oncology trials.²–⁶ ￼

These disparities are often attributed to structural racism, and they are.
But beneath that structural layer lies a technical one.

In communities where coding is inconsistent, incomplete, or under-resourced, patients become invisible to the very systems intended to serve them. Their diagnoses are under-coded. Their tumor subtypes are incompletely captured. Their imaging follow-up is inconsistently documented. Their recurrence risk is never algorithmically calculated. Their eligibility for advanced therapies or clinical trials is never triggered.⁴–⁶ ￼

The billable code does not just describe reality.
It shapes who gets access to precision.

And what it cannot see, the system cannot correct.

⸻

## The Code as the Genome of Health-Care AI

When artificial intelligence entered medicine, the billable code found a new role. It became the genome of every model. Machine-learning systems trained on EHR data inherit the coded structure of those records — and therefore inherit every unevenness, every omission, every historical inequity.

Several widely cited analyses — in Science and leading ethics and medical journals — have demonstrated that AI models trained on coded encounter data can perform systematically worse for minoritized patients, not because the models themselves are explicitly biased, but because the underlying coded data disproportionately reflects the care of White, insured, and continuously treated populations.¹,⁷ ￼

The AI learns the world that the billable code describes.
And the billable code does not describe everyone equally.

This realization was part of what drove me, years later, to build MammoChat and the broader Oncology Nexus. Not simply as a technical project, but as an ethical one.

If precision medicine depends on structured data, and if structured data depends on the billable code, then equity requires something deeper than code optimization. It requires provenance — the ability to track who creates the data, who uses it, and who benefits.

Because without provenance, the billable code remains an instrument of extraction.

With provenance, it becomes a tool of justice.
