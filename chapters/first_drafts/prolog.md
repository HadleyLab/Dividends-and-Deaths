# PROLOGUE — The First Dividend


On a winter morning in 1951, a thirty-one-year-old Black woman walked into Johns Hopkins Hospital with a pain she could no longer ignore. Henrietta Lacks—daughter of tobacco farmers, mother of five, a woman whose life had already been shaped by segregation and poverty—was examined, biopsied, and diagnosed with cervical cancer. She knew the tumor was there. She did not know that part of it would be taken again, without her knowledge, for research.

In the lab, something unprecedented happened. The cells taken from her tumor—not the healthy tissue, not a mix of both, but the cancer itself—refused to die. They divided every 24 hours with astonishing regularity. In an era when most human cells survived only briefly outside the body, Henrietta’s cells grew indefinitely. They became the first immortal human cell line, later named HeLa.¹

HeLa cells went on to underpin a century’s worth of biomedical breakthroughs: from the groundwork for the polio vaccine to studies that led to HPV vaccines, cancer drugs, HIV medications, in-vitro fertilization, and even early work on SARS-CoV-2.²⁻⁴ They became the workhorse of modern cell biology, quietly powering experiments in thousands of labs around the world. Today, more than 75,000 scientific papers cite HeLa cells.⁴

In economic terms, those cells have helped generate trillions of dollars in downstream value: vaccines, biologics, diagnostics, devices, patents, and careers. But Henrietta Lacks never signed a consent form. Her family did not know her cells had been taken. They did not know those cells were being sold, shipped, and experimented on worldwide. They did not share in any of that value.

Henrietta died in October 1951. Her children grew up in poverty, often uninsured, and for decades could not afford the very medical advances her cells helped create. The first time some family members learned the truth, it was because a researcher called them to draw blood—without explaining that their mother’s cells were already immortal and famous.¹

Henrietta Lacks never agreed to fund the rise of precision medicine.
But economically, that is exactly what she did.

HeLa is not just the beginning of modern cell culture. It is the first recorded dividend in the precision medicine economy—a dividend extracted from a Black woman’s body, multiplied across generations of scientific profit, and never returned.

Decades later, the legal system finally began to catch up. In 2023, Henrietta’s descendants reached a confidential settlement with Thermo Fisher Scientific, one of multiple companies alleged to have profited from HeLa-derived products without consent.⁵⁻⁷ More recently, the family has brought similar claims against other firms that used HeLa cells in profitable drug development.⁸ These cases are more than lawsuits; they are a signal that the economic logic underpinning modern biomedicine is finally on trial.

As a physician-scientist, an NIH-funded genomics and AI researcher, and a founder of a precision-health company, I have spent my career inside the systems built on top of that first stolen dividend. I have watched how we turn bodies into data, data into codes, and codes into revenue. I have seen the gap between who creates value and who captures it.

That gap is not an accident. It is the operating model.

⸻

Today, we call it precision medicine when we tailor treatment to the molecular profile of a tumor or a genome. The phrase conjures images of fairness and scientific elegance: the right drug for the right patient at the right time. But when you follow the money, a different picture emerges.

In the United States, every lab test, biopsy, mammogram, and chemotherapy infusion is translated into billable codes—CPT, ICD, DRG. Those codes do not just document care; they define the economic unit of the system. The precision medicine economy is built on this translation layer. A woman’s breast tissue becomes a pathology report, which becomes a code, which becomes revenue for hospitals, payors, pharmaceutical companies, and AI vendors building tools on top of those data.

The patient generates all the value.
The patient rarely shares in the dividend.

Nowhere is this more visible than in breast cancer.

Over the past several decades, breast cancer mortality has dropped by roughly 43% in the United States, a remarkable achievement attributed to earlier detection and better treatment. Yet during the same period, a stubborn and devastating pattern has remained: **Black women are about 40% more likely to die of breast cancer than White women, despite having slightly lower incidence.**⁹⁻¹¹ This is not a marginal effect. It is a structural one. Breast cancer is now the leading cause of cancer death among Black women.¹²

High-impact reviews in CA: A Cancer Journal for Clinicians, NEJM, JAMA Oncology, and The Lancet converge on the same conclusion: differences in tumor biology explain only part of this disparity. The rest is driven by delayed diagnosis, under-treatment, gaps in access to high-quality care, and structural racism embedded in where clinics are sited, who gets screened, and which patients are offered advanced therapies or clinical trials.⁹⁻¹³

When we look at precision oncology, the inequity becomes even sharper. Meta-analyses of precision oncology trials show that non-Hispanic White patients are consistently over-represented, while Black, Hispanic, and many Asian and Indigenous populations are under-represented, sometimes by half relative to their disease burden.¹⁴⁻¹⁶ Real-world precision oncology datasets and scoping reviews on structural racism reach the same conclusion: the more “precise” our science becomes, the more it risks excluding the very populations with the highest mortality.¹⁵⁻¹⁷

In other words:
The dividends of precision medicine flow to those inside the datasets.
The deaths accumulate among those outside them.

This is the core thesis of this book.

⸻

The story of Henrietta Lacks is often told as a historical injustice that medicine is slowly, painfully learning to correct. That is true—but incomplete. Her story is also a template for a much larger problem: an economic architecture that depends on extracting biological value from marginalized communities without guaranteeing them any share of the benefits.

That architecture did not end in 1951. It evolved.

In our time, a new extractive engine has emerged: the circular AI economy. Here, unpaid human data—our search histories, social media posts, digital records, and even clinical notes—are used to train large-scale AI models. Those models become multi-billion-dollar assets that require massive compute, specialized chips, and tight corporate partnerships. Recent reporting has described how AI companies, cloud providers, and chip manufacturers form circular deals that pour profits back into the same small set of actors at the center of the system. The human contributors whose data made these models possible receive no direct dividend.

If that sounds familiar, it should. AI is replaying the economic logic of precision medicine—only faster, bigger, and with fewer constraints.

Here is the irony: what remains largely unregulated in AI has already been constrained in medicine. Over the past thirty years, healthcare has quietly developed one of the most sophisticated data-rights frameworks in the world: HIPAA, HITECH, the 21st Century Cures Act, and the Information Blocking Rule. These laws are far from perfect, but they exist for a single reason: to ensure that no one’s data can be used the way Henrietta’s was—without consent, transparency, or recourse.

Medicine, for all its failures, has been forced to build guardrails.
AI, so far, has not.

Yet in clinics across the country, I see another truth: even with these guardrails, the lived experience of many patients still feels like Henrietta’s. They are scanned, biopsied, sequenced, and surveilled. Their data lives forever in hospital systems, registries, and corporate warehouses. They rarely know where it goes. They almost never know what it’s worth.

When a woman dies of breast cancer in America today, the odds are unacceptably high that she is not White.⁹⁻¹³ That is not an inevitable outcome of biology. It is the predictable result of an economy that treats some people’s data as a commodity and some people’s lives as expendable.

⸻

This book is my attempt to name that economy and to show that it can be rewritten.

I write as someone who has mined clinical data at scale, built models on top of it, won federal grants to do so, and now leads a company—MammoChat.com — Your Journey, Together. I have seen both sides: the promise of precision medicine and the quiet ledger of who pays the price. Through MammoChat and the broader provenance-first ecosystem I call the Oncology Nexus, my team and I are building tools that start from a different premise: patients should own the story their data tells, and they should share in the dividends it creates.

In the chapters that follow, we will move from Henrietta Lacks to 23andMe, from breast cancer wards to AI data centers, from billing codes to blockchain ledgers. We will follow the money, the methods, and the laws. We will ask how much has been taken, from whom, and how we might—finally—begin to pay it back.

Henrietta’s cells helped launch the precision medicine economy.
The question now is whether her legacy can help us reclaim it.
