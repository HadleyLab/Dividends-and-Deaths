# CHAPTER THREE — DEATHS: WHERE THE BURDEN FALLS

When I began working in genomics and clinical data, I thought I knew what inequity looked like. I had seen the skewed datasets, the missing codes, the fragmented care pathways that erased entire communities from the narrative of precision medicine. But it was not until I turned my attention to mortality—the final, unforgiving metric—that I understood the full weight of what had been built.

Breast cancer is where this inequity becomes impossible to ignore. Black women are roughly 40% more likely to die of breast cancer than White women, despite lower incidence (CA Cancer J Clin, DeSantis et al. 2022)¹. This statistic has been repeated in NEJM (Daly & Olopade 2022)², JAMA Oncology, and The Lancet. No serious dataset contradicts it. There is no dispute in the literature.

And yet, breast cancer is one of the most studied diseases in human history.
It is not an orphan condition.
It is not rare.
It is not technologically neglected.
It is not underfunded.

If precision medicine were working as intended, this should be the disease where inequities shrink. Instead, they widen.

This is not biology.
This is architecture.

⸻

The UCSF Revelation — Diseases Don’t Come One at a Time

At UCSF, I worked down the hall from Esteban González Burchard, one of the clearest voices in genomic inequity. His work made something plain: minority patients do not experience disease one condition at a time, and yet precision medicine—built on reductive models—pretends they do.

Burchard’s research showed:
	•	Black and Latino children suffer the highest asthma mortality rates in the country (Pediatrics, Akinbami et al. 2012; Chest 2014, Burchard et al.)³
	•	Puerto Rican children have the highest asthma prevalence in the world, nearly 3× that of white children (PNAS 2015, Nishimura & Burchard)⁴
	•	African ancestry is associated with worse asthma exacerbations, even when controlling for environment (JACI 2015, Kumar et al.)⁵
	•	The genetic variants that drive asthma risk in minorities are often absent in Eurocentric GWAS datasets, making clinical prediction tools less accurate for the people who need them most (NEJM 2016, Popejoy & Fullerton)⁶

These were not modest findings. They were indictments.

Burchard also demonstrated something even more fundamental:
most minority patients experience comorbidities that Eurocentric precision medicine never models together.

Asthma and obesity.
Asthma and preterm birth.
Asthma and exposure to pollution concentrated in redlined neighborhoods.
Asthma and stress from systemic racism.
Asthma and hypertension.
Asthma and diabetes.

Eurocentric precision medicine saw one disease.
Patients lived with five.

The same pattern holds across conditions:

Hypertension

Black Americans develop hypertension earlier and with greater severity.
Yet the foundational genetic risk models come almost exclusively from European ancestry biobanks (Nature 2018, Hoffmann et al.)⁷.

Diabetes

Latino, Native, and Black patients have significantly higher rates of diabetes and earlier complications.
Yet the clinical prediction algorithms (HbA1c thresholds, progression risk scoring) are least accurate for them (JAMA 2021, Osei et al.)⁸.

Pregnancy & Preterm Birth

Black women face a 50% higher risk of preterm birth, even after controlling for income, insurance, and education (NEJM 2020, Taylor et al.)⁹.

Kidney Disease

APOL1 variants dramatically increase CKD risk for African ancestry patients—variants that were unknown for decades simply because no one sequenced enough African ancestry genomes (NEJM 2010, Genovese et al.)¹⁰.

COVID-19

Black Americans died at twice the rate of White Americans early in the pandemic.
Latino mortality climbed 4–5× in several states.
Native communities suffered devastation unmatched in modern U.S. epidemiology (Lancet 2021, Hill et al.)¹¹.

These are not separate problems.
They are the same problem: a healthcare system built on data from populations least affected by disease.

⸻

The Multiplicative Nature of Inequity

When you place these conditions on top of each other, something startling emerges. For many minority patients, risk is not additive—it is multiplicative.

Asthma × pollution × poor access to specialty care × housing instability × hypertension × diabetes × lack of genomic testing = an entirely different disease trajectory.

Yet the models used to predict risk—polygenic scores, machine-learning prognostics, EHR-based algorithms—were built on the assumption of a single disease in a single body.

As Burchard put it in one of his most cited papers:

“Precision medicine for the few is not precision medicine.”
—Esteban González Burchard, NEJM, 2016 (Popejoy & Fullerton)⁶

The phrase could serve as the epitaph of the first generation of precision medicine.

⸻

The Geography of Absence

When I began doing breast cancer work in Florida, it became obvious how deeply these inequities ran. Breast cancer mortality was just the surface layer.

Underneath were layers of comorbidity—hypertension, diabetes, delayed screening, inconsistent imaging, environmental exposures, and the coded architecture of clinics that served as gatekeepers to precision therapies.

Women with dense coded histories were eligible for genomic recurrence scoring.
Women with fragmented histories were not.

Women who received timely follow-up after an abnormal screening mammogram lived significantly longer (Cancer 2020, Fedewa et al.)¹².
Women who waited months—often due to transportation, work schedules, insurance churn—died more often.

The mortality curve was not determined by tumor biology.
It was determined by data completeness.

The more complete your data, the longer you lived.
The less complete your data, the higher your risk of dying from a disease we already know how to treat.

This is why precision medicine “works” on paper:
the people with the best access to healthcare also have the most data.
The people with the most data also get the most precision.
And therefore the best outcomes.

The science looks impressive because the pipeline selects its own winners.

⸻

Clinical Trials — Where Future Deaths Are Decided

Precision oncology trials determine who will receive the next generation of therapy. They are the mechanism by which the future becomes real.

And they are massively inequitable.

A JAMA Network Open analysis (Loree et al., 2021) shows that:
	•	Black patients are 40–60% underrepresented
	•	Hispanic patients are 50% underrepresented
	•	Asian and Indigenous patients are drastically underrepresented

These disparities exist even in trials for diseases that disproportionately affect minority groups.

A NEJM review (Popejoy & Fullerton 2016) found that:

81% of all genome-wide association study participants are of European ancestry.
African ancestry participants represent <2%.
Latino and Indigenous ancestry combined: <1%.

This is not lack of effort.
It is lack of economic incentive.

Recruitment is easiest where the system already functions.
Everywhere else becomes expensive.

I learned that lesson viscerally during my decentralized COVID-19 imaging trial at UCF.

⸻

COVID-19 — The Exposing Event

When the pandemic shut down clinical research, the inequities I had spent decades studying erupted in plain sight. Social determinants of health—usually relegated to grant introductions—became front-page news.

Black Americans died at nearly twice the rate of White Americans in early phases of the pandemic (Lancet 2021, Hill et al.)¹¹.
Latino mortality rates reached catastrophic highs in some states.
Native communities saw mortality ratios higher than anything in modern American epidemiology.

I quickly shifted my research and launched one of the earliest decentralized imaging-data trials in the country—NCT05384912 (Hadley et al., 2022)¹³. Patients could upload chest imaging from home or hospital portals. The trial informed my book chapter on COVID-19 and the emerging “metaverse of medical data.”

But the most telling discovery came from our recruitment experiment.

We used Facebook ads to recruit participants.
The cost difference was staggering:
	•	Under $200 to recruit a White woman
	•	Over $9,000 to recruit a Black woman

A 45× cost differential for the same digital outreach.

This was inequity in its purest form—no biology, no clinics, no radiologists, no insurance companies. Just a platform and an algorithm revealing the price of reaching people the system had long ignored.

The pandemic showed how devastating inequity could be.
But it also revealed something else.

The COVID-19 vaccine trials—for all their flaws—were among the most equitable trials in American history.
Not because the system suddenly learned equity,
but because the entire country needed the vaccine.
Everyone had to be included or the economy could not reopen.

It was the rare moment where survival demanded inclusivity.

Once the crisis passed, inequity returned—this time wearing digital clothing.

⸻

The Arithmetic of Death

The precision-medicine economy measures success by innovation:
new drugs, new biomarkers, new AI models.

But death measures something else:
the distribution of that innovation.

Death accumulates where:
	•	comorbidities cluster
	•	environments degrade health
	•	data disappears
	•	codes go missing
	•	follow-up lapses
	•	trust erodes
	•	recruitment fails
	•	economics block access
	•	history repeats itself

Death accumulates exactly where the system has not been designed to see.

When I look at the mortality curves today—in breast cancer, in asthma, in heart disease, in diabetes, in COVID—I see the reflection of two decades of datasets I analyzed, engineered, or inherited.

I see my patients.
I see the women of West Philadelphia.
I see the families of San Francisco’s Mission District.
I see the neighborhoods of Central Florida.

And I see my daughters—
standing at the intersection of every underrepresented ancestry,
every structural barrier,
every algorithmic blind spot,
every coded omission.

The precision-medicine economy was never abstract to me after that.

It was personal.

The rest of this book is about what we must do—structurally, economically, and morally—to ensure the next generation of precision medicine does not replicate the inequities of the last.
