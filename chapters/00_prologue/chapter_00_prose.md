# Prologue

 

## The Mother of Precision Medicine

 

In every hospital I’ve ever worked in, there is a woman who never leaves.

 

She is not in a bed.

She is not on the ward.

She is in the freezer.

 

Her cells live in tiny labeled vials, stacked in metal racks, dipped into liquid nitrogen and pulled out again and again. They survive radiation, toxins, viruses, and time, dividing when other cells die. They have outlived the doctors who first grew them, the nurses who cared for the woman they came from, and the generations of scientists who built their careers on her biology.

 

Her name is Henrietta Lacks, and she is the mother of precision medicine.

 

In 1951, in a segregated ward at Johns Hopkins, a Black woman in her early thirties lay on a metal table while a surgeon shaved off a piece of her cervical tumor. No one asked for her consent. No one explained that her cells would be taken for research. In the lab downstairs, those cells did something no human cells had done before: they refused to die. They doubled every twenty-four hours, filling flask after flask. They became the first immortal human cell line—HeLa—and they are still growing today.

 

HeLa cells helped develop the polio vaccine. They helped map the human genome. They helped test chemotherapies, HIV drugs, HPV (human papillomavirus) vaccines, and gene editing. They built patents, companies, and fortunes.

 

Henrietta’s cells became currency.

Henrietta never saw a cent.

That fact still tightens my chest.

 

The dividends from her body flowed upward—into journals, pipelines, IPOs.

The deaths and the burden stayed where they always had—downstream, in families like hers.

In Chapter 1, I will give that currency its formal name: the Billable Code. The prologue’s job is to show you the cost before we start diagramming the machinery.

 

I was trained in that world. I helped build companies and algorithms there. It has fed my career and broken my heart. I still feel that break every time I return to these stories. And everything I am about to say in this book—about who gets paid, who gets studied, who gets seen, and who gets left to die quietly—starts somewhere else, on two small Caribbean islands where my parents learned what sacrifice really means.



## Behind God’s Back

 

My father, Leonard Kempthorne, was born in a fishing village called Charlotteville, at the far northeastern tip of Tobago. When he was a boy there were no roads in or out. You reached it by boat or you didn’t reach it at all. The bay was a perfect crescent—turquoise water, white sand, dense green hills—and behind those hills lay about 900 acres commissioned to Francis Hadley, a Major in the British army, for his service to the Crown.

 

One of the coves on that land is now called Englishman’s Bay, famous on postcards and travel blogs. As a child, my father’s world was not postcards. It was saltfish, school, church, and whatever the sea and the hills would give.

 

Up on the ridge, the Major Hadley’s son came down into the village and “troubled” a young Black woman named Veronica—my grandmother. He returned to Trinidad, married someone more acceptable, and founded a “proper” family.

 

My father was the outside child.

 

He and his half sister, Betty, were raised by Veronica alone. We called her Granny. She was a master seamstress. She could take a flat piece of fabric and, with a few cuts and stitches, turn it into something that fit a body perfectly. She did the same with her child: patched together scraps of opportunity and discipline into a life.

 

Charlotteville was poor. As my father liked to say, “We were poor, but we never knew it.” Everyone was poor. Everyone knew everyone. If you misbehaved in the street, any adult could beat you. When they brought you home, your mother beat you again. It was brutal and honest and communal—a village in the oldest sense of the word.

 

Granny met the limits of that village head-on. One day, Seventh-day Adventist missionaries arrived with a proposition: a place at a boarding school in Trinidad called Caribbean Union College. There was no road out of Charlotteville yet. So she put her teenage son on a boat with the missionaries and sent him away, trusting that God and strangers would do what the island could not.

 

No road.

No safety net.

Just a boy, a boat, and a mother’s faith.

 

He never lived in Charlotteville again.

Colonial extraction left only one path out, even when the island had none.



## The Rose from Gasparillo

 

My mother’s story began in Gasparillo, in South Trinidad—cane country and oil country, where Indian indenture met British extraction. She was born Surajee, one of ten surviving siblings. A few more died in childbirth, at home, before anyone could name them. That was normal.

 

Her family was Hindu. In that world, sons might dream of becoming doctors; daughters became teachers or nurses—respectable, useful, limited.

 

At sixteen, she left Trinidad for England to train as a nurse. Imagine that: a teenage girl from a village of cane fields, dropped into the brick and fog of the NHS (National Health Service). She came home altered. She had a starched nurse’s uniform and memories she rarely spoke about, and somewhere along the way she had added a new first name: Pearl. “Surajee” was too foreign, too Indian for the British wards. “Pearl” was easier for everyone else’s tongue.

 

Colonialism does not only redraw maps. It rewrites names.

 

If she had been born a few decades later, she might have been a doctor. In her time, nursing was the ceiling for a girl like her. But she hit that ceiling hard, and then pushed past it in other ways.

 

Back in Trinidad, she took a job at a small Seventh-day Adventist community hospital. One day, the new hospital administrator arrived: a young man from Tobago, fresh back from Andrews University in Michigan, the Harvard of Adventist higher education. He had grown up poor in Charlotteville, the outside child of a British-descended line and a Black seamstress. He had now returned as “Brother Kempthorne,” with an American degree and a preacher’s voice.

 

She ran the ward.

He ran the hospital.

They noticed each other.

 

He used to joke, years later, that he went “down South to pick the most beautiful rose” from among the Indian families in Gasparillo. My mother would laugh and say, “If my father was alive, I could never marry you.” She wasn’t exaggerating. In her world, marrying a creole—a Black man—was considered shameful, dangerous, unthinkable.

 

None of her older relatives attended the wedding in protest. Two of her younger brothers did as did her brother-in-law. She converted to Adventism, not because she forgot who she was, but because she needed a bridge between the Hindu village of her birth and the Christian village she was about to join.

 

He brought a village where everyone knew your name.

She brought a village where everyone knew your story.

 

Together, they created something else entirely.



## Malta, Mangoes, and Mauby

 

I grew up inside that something.

 

We had three grandmothers and one grandfather in our emotional orbit: Mama in Gasparillo; Granny, my Black Tobagonian grandmother; Grandma, my Venezuelan step‑grandmother; and Grandpa, the Trinidadian son of the British Major’s line.

 

On some weekends, my mother drove us from our home in St. James in Port of Spain “down South” to Gasparillo to see Mama, who lived with her elder sister, one of the main ringleaders in the family opposing her marrying that creole boy. She would jokingly explain it away and say once the family saw my brother and me with “straight hair” they accepted us. Again, I suspect she was right as we were never treated differently for being dougla. However, my father never went down south with us. Just my mother, my brother, and me—straight hair and all.

I only knew Mama senile. She never aged to me either. She was always old until one day she died. She would go about her day in her white oorni or Hindu headpiece, a simple white dress, and thick black horn-rimmed glasses worthy of a nonagenarian. She had a frail body always chanting in Hindi—her daily prayers or poojas, singing her bhajans. Up until I emigrated, she was still doing her normal chores like washing her clothes and everything else slightly out of her mind.

Mama was so old they didn’t know her exact age. Her birth certificate was lost in the Red House fire of 1903 when she was a little girl. As she could neither read nor write it was understandable they didn’t know her exact date of birth. We assumed she died close to 100 years old. I distinctly remember returning to Trinidad that Christmas break of my first year in medical school for the funeral.

Her body was cremated in traditional Hindu pomp and ceremony like the matriarch she was. Her body burned in an outdoor pyre on the banks of the village creek used for such ceremonies. Her ashes returned to the ground that bore her as she was celebrated by the community for her lifelong contributions.

Care was honored in ritual because the economy never counted it.

Her big yard was our little universe: sugar cane along the back fence, mango trees of every variety, pawpaw, guava, governor plums, regular plums, whatever could grow in Trinidad’s heat. There were always cousins. Always some uncle tuning a car. 

My brother and I climbed trees with our cousins, made slingshots out of old tire tubes and forked branches, tried and failed to hit birds, fed ducks over the fence with leftover roti, and busted bamboo for Divali by the river in the village. Medicine there was bush medicine: teas, poultices, home remedies, and a community that watched you even when you didn’t know it was watching.

 

Other weekends, we went to River Estate, where Granny had moved after leaving Tobago to be closer to her son. Her yard was different but no less rich: sugar cane, coconut trees, a few other fruit trees, and the small plants she fussed over. Granny worked as a seamstress at the Adventist community hospital where my father was administrator—he made sure of that. She took public transport everywhere, small and sturdy and quiet, never complaining.

 

When my father went to cut her grass, there was always one cold Malta in the fridge that was reserved for my brother and me. Not two—one. We’d open the door, take out the single brown glass bottle, and split it exactly into two glasses. She didn’t make a big show of it. No speeches. That was the point. 

 

Sometimes she would have a small jug of mauby as well, that bitter bark drink that somehow tasted like childhood and adulthood at the same time, sweet with brown sugar like  back in Charlotteville. But it was that single Malta that was her way of saying “I love you, I see you,” without ever needing the words. To this day, I can feel the weight of that bottle in my hands, the clink of the glasses, the unspoken ritual of being cherished.

 

Granny lived alone in Trinidad. Strong, Black, and fiercely independent. Sometimes, when she came to visit us in St. James, she’d bring Tobagonian sweets—sugar cake, bene balls, tulum, little hard candies wrapped in plastic. It felt like she carried a piece of Charlotteville in her handbag.

 

Then on the holidays, especially Boxing Day, we went to Barataria, to my father’s step-family. There, we sat at tables laid out by Grandma Anita, the Venezuelan matriarch, and Grandpa Frank, my father’s Trinidadian ‘old man’ whose father had once been that British Major Hadley commissioned with over 900 acres back in Charlotteville. My father had long reconciled with them as a young man in Trinidad. We grew up knowing we were part of that family too, even if our place in it had begun as scandal.

 

My brother and I were Dougla—African and Indian mixed. At home, we were Seventh-day Adventists. For school, my father sent us to Fatima College, a Catholic “prestige school,” because in his world education always came first. As fate would have it, the school shared a namesake with my future wife, completely unbeknownst to me at the time. Sabbaths we spent in church. Weekdays we sat under statues of the Virgin Mary and studied physics, chemistry, and biology. 

 

It was messy and layered and absolutely normal to me:

mangoes and Malta and mauby, Hindu weddings and Adventist potlucks, sugar cane in the yard and Latin on the report card.

Those weekends were my first accounting lessons. I watched how love, labor, and lineage were distributed—who received the cold Malta, who never left with an empty bag of fruit, who quietly absorbed the cost when the dividends of the family ran out. I did not have the language for extraction yet, but I understood that value always moved through people before it hardened into systems. I felt it before I could explain it. That is the ledger I carry forward into this book.



## A Long Line of Universal Men

 

My father had a particular phrase he used with my brother and me: “We come from a long line of universal men.” One line ran from a British Major through a Trinidadian son and a Venezuelan wife into us. Another ran from Indian indentured laborers through cane cutters and oil workers, through my mother and her decision to leave at sixteen, into us. My father used to say he was proud of this chaos. He would tell us his life could not fit into a single box, and that he liked it that way.

 

Despite all these seemingly insurmountable obstacles, my parents built a life anyway.

 

Later, when Granny grew older and needed more care, she moved to Trinidad. When my father and I eventually left for America, she couldn’t follow us—England was still part of the Commonwealth in a way the United States was not. So she went to live with her daughter there, who was, of course, also a nurse, my aunty Betty. Both Granny and Aunty Betty died in England. My father’s life stretched across three islands and a continent, held together by strong women’s courage and a seamstress’s stubbornness.



## Code and Calling

 

Somewhere in the middle of all of that, another story was forming quietly.

 

When I was about ten years old, a family friend who had migrated left behind an ancient Tandy TRS‑80. By the time it reached our house in Trinidad, it was already a relic. To me, it was magic. I taught myself to program on that machine, typing BASIC commands from its manuals and watching the blinking cursor obey. I didn’t have the language for it then, but I was learning to think in systems—mapping states, transitions, causes, and effects. The feeling was pure agency.

 

Later, my aunty Betty in England—the same nurse who took Granny in—sent me my first IBM 286 clone. Beige, noisy, and perfect. That machine felt like a portal. I spent hours writing code, breaking it, fixing it, learning how small, precise instructions could bend an entire system.

 

At the same time, there was never really a question about what I was going to be. With a Hindu-born Indian mother who had become Pearl the nurse in colonial England and then a ward sister in Trinidad, the highest thing her Dougla son could be was obvious: doctor.

 

My middle name, Devanand, came from a Bollywood actor. My destiny, in her mind, came from a longer script: her sacrifices, her migration, her name change, her marriage. I would be the doctor she never had a chance to become.

 

By my teen years, two forces had locked into place:

- I loved writing software, understanding systems, making machines behave.
- I was going to be a doctor, because that’s what you do when your mother is named Surajee and has spent her life caring for other people’s children in other people’s hospitals.

 

I didn’t want to choose between them.

I wanted to practice medicine in code.



## Migration and the Cost of Opportunity

 

By the time I was around thirteen, my mother and older brother had already left for the United States. It was the era when American hospitals were desperate for nurses because of the growing HIV epidemic. Trinidad and Tobago was still part of the old colonial staffing pipeline: nurses trained in England, moved back to the Caribbean, and then onward to the U.S.

 

My father and I followed later. By then my parents were in their fifties, starting over in Orlando. They left behind their house, their communities, and everything they knew so that my brother and I could have access to a kind of education and opportunities that simply did not exist on small islands.

 

We like to talk about opportunity as if it is free. It isn’t. It is paid for in grandparents left behind, graves dug in foreign soil, and mothers who add new names to survive in new places.



## Franklin’s University and the Promise of Precision

 

I eventually landed at the University of Pennsylvania—Benjamin Franklin’s university, the only Ivy League school not named after a person because Franklin, a Quaker, argued that universities should exist to serve people, not immortalize donors. Quaker meetinghouses prize simplicity and equality; that cultural stamp sits under Penn’s founding story.

 

I started as a straight medical student. But the pull of data was too strong.

 

Penn was launching an MD/PhD program in Genomics and Computational Biology—a combined medical and research doctorate in DNA-scale biology and computation—and I joined the inaugural class. The Human Genome Project had just produced its reference sequence. We talked about it as if it were the human genome—singular, definitive. Only later would it become widely acknowledged that the sequence came largely from individuals of European ancestry. Precise, yes. But not accurate for most of the world.

 

We still called it “personalized medicine” then. The idea was seductive: use DNA to tailor treatments to each individual. A few years later, when Barack Obama announced the Precision Medicine Initiative, the word shifted. “Precision” sounded harder, more scientific, more exact—as if medicine could become a sniper rifle instead of a shotgun.

 

But a sniper rifle aimed at the wrong population is still missing the target.

 

At CHOP—the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia—I had my first real encounter with what that meant. I worked with Hakon Hakonarson at the newly formed Center for Applied Genomics (CAG). It was one of the most ambitious pediatric genomics centers in the world. They were genotyping tens of thousands of children and wiring their DNA to rich clinical data.

 

CHOP sits in West Philadelphia, a predominantly Black neighborhood. Kids from that community fill its emergency rooms, its clinics, its waiting rooms.

 

From that operation, we helped build NeuroFix Therapeutics, a precision medicine startup focused on attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and impulsivity. I was the lead bioinformatician, translating DNA and clinical data into usable models. We used CAG’s data—tens of thousands of genotyped children, their DNA variations cataloged—to stratify complex behavioral traits, design a trial, and execute it at a speed that would have been unthinkable in the old era of paper charts and Rolodexes.

 

CAG had re-contact superpowers. Families had broadly consented to be called back. The infrastructure was in place. What had once taken a decade and a billion dollars—a pediatric trial—could now be sketched in a few years and less than a million.

 

It felt like magic.

 

Then we looked closely at who was in the dataset.

 

The overwhelming majority of those children were White.

 

CHOP is wrapped by Black neighborhoods. But the genomic engine powering our discoveries barely touched them. It wasn’t CHOP’s “fault” in any simple sense. They studied the families who came, who had insurance, who could take time off work, who had cars. The pipeline into the data was already biased long before we started modeling anything.

 

The dividends of that dataset flowed upward:

papers, patents, startup valuations, faculty positions, keynote talks.

 

The deaths—undiagnosed ADHD in under-resourced schools, comorbidities worsened by stress and pollution, decades of missed opportunity—stayed in the neighborhoods around the hospital.

 

Henrietta’s story, updated for the era of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and ADHD.

Part I will quantify those flows; here I only needed you to feel how familiar the pattern already was.



## The Transitional Year and the Decision Not to Cut

 

After medical school, I did a Transitional Year in surgery at the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania. Part of me imagined becoming a surgeon: the person who physically harvested the tissue that genomics would later decode.

 

It took less than a year to understand two things:

1.    Surgeons are doing holy work.

2.    I did not want to do that work for the rest of my life.

 

I saw everything: fecal disimpactions, surgical abscesses, overnight codes, death in fluorescent light. I scrubbed in, I cut, I sutured, I rounded, I stood in line for midnight coffee feeling like I had been scraped out and left hollow. I respected the craft. I also knew I did not want to spend my life living at the edge of a scalpel.

 

I realized something that felt like a confession:

 

I loved medicine too much to practice it inside a system I already knew was broken.

 

Being a good doctor, in the traditional sense, meant helping twenty or thirty patients a day. Being the kind of doctor my parents had made me—part coder, part village child, part systems thinker—meant I might help millions if I could fix the infrastructure instead of just working within it.

 

So I didn’t become a surgeon.

I turned toward code.



## Stanford, Ontologies, and the Skeleton of the System

 

At Stanford, I entered residency in Clinical Pathology and Laboratory Medicine. If Penn and CHOP taught me how to read the genome, Stanford taught me how to read the code that runs medicine itself.

 

In pathology, nothing moves without a label:

- LOINC codes for lab tests
- SNOMED codes for diseases and tissues
- ICD codes for diagnoses
- CPT codes for billable procedures

These are the standardized codebooks that turn care into billing and data.

 

Upstairs, people like Mark Musen were standardizing these into formal ontologies—controlled vocabularies for machines—and decision-support systems. Downstairs, we were signing out biopsies and lab reports that would be translated into those codes, sent to insurers, and used to justify payments.

 

It hit me with a clarity I could not unsee:

 

The Billable Code is the fundamental economic unit of American healthcare.

 

If something is not coded, it might as well not exist—for reimbursement, for analytics, for research, for AI (machine-learning systems). That is true whether we are talking about a breast biopsy, a metastasis (cancer spread), a recurrence (its return), or a clinical trial response.

 

Around that time, I met Atul Butte, who led Systems Medicine at Stanford. He focused on the data already sitting in hospital servers, insurance warehouses, and public repositories—the biggest untapped resource in medicine.

 

He looked at my CHOP experience, my work with Hakon and NeuroFix, my PhD in genomics and computational biology, and he did something simple and life-changing: he said, “Come work with me.”

 

I quit residency after the first year. I wasn’t walking away from medicine. I was walking towards a different way of practicing it.



## UCSF, Eighty Million Lives, and the Profit of Failure

 

When Atul was recruited to UCSF to build what would become the Bakar Institute for Computational Health Sciences, backed by a major gift from Mark Zuckerberg, I followed as faculty. I never even filled out a formal application. One day I was at Stanford. The next, I was part of a team wiring together data from across the entire University of California health system.

 

Tens of millions of patient records. Years of lab results, diagnoses, prescriptions, outcomes. We mined open datasets like the Gene Expression Omnibus, layered them over clinical codes, and started building models: predicting who would get sepsis, the body-wide spiral of infection, which tumors would respond to which drugs, who would die in the hospital, who could be safely sent home.

 

I co-authored a deep learning model, a neural-network system, that could detect Alzheimer’s disease on FDG-PET scans, a metabolic imaging test, years before the diagnosis showed up in the chart. On paper, it was a triumph of precision: the ability to see neurodegeneration long before the clinical exam caught it.

 

At home, my mother was already drifting into moderate Alzheimer’s.

 

That is the quiet heartbreak of this work. You can help the system see patterns earlier and still watch your own family slip through gaps that should not exist.

 

The more data we analyzed, the more I saw something that made me angry in a way I still haven’t fully metabolized:

 

**Health systems profit from failing their patients.**

 

We saw it in claims and codes and trajectories:

- Every readmission is revenue.
- Every extra dialysis session is revenue.
- Every ICU (intensive care unit) day is revenue.
- A metastatic cancer is more profitable than a cured one.

 

At the bedside, nurses and doctors fight like hell for their patients. But the system—the spreadsheets, the budgets, the contracts—tells a different story.

 

Henrietta’s cells built an empire that never paid her family.

Hospital data does the same, daily, for millions of people who have never heard of the models built on top of their lives.



## Florida, DEI, and a Pacemaker Serial Number

 

In 2019, I left California and moved to Florida because my parents needed me more than my CV did.

 

My mother’s Alzheimer’s was no longer subtle. She repeated herself. She got confused and angry. She lost whole afternoons. My father’s hypertension and diabetes had progressed to end-stage kidney disease. He was on dialysis and eventually had a pacemaker implanted.

 

I became Chief of AI at the University of Central Florida’s medical school. On paper, Florida seemed like a dream for precision medicine: the state is incredibly diverse—Black, Latino, Caribbean, immigrant, rural, urban. All the people left out of CHOP’s datasets live here.

 

But data in Florida was fractured. The University of Central Florida (UCF), HCA, AdventHealth, and others all had their own systems, their own politics, their own codes. Unlike California’s unified UC system or CHOP’s dominance over a region, Florida had no coherent data backbone.

 

Then COVID-19 hit.

 

Mammography units shut down. Routine cancer screening went dark. We pivoted and launched a trial—later registered as NCT05384912—to crowdsource COVID chest imaging directly from patients over the internet. We bought ads on Facebook to recruit people willing to upload their X-rays and CT scans. That’s when we discovered another brutal number: it cost hundreds of dollars in ad spend to recruit a White woman to donate a single chest image. To recruit a Black woman? Thousands of dollars.

 

Diversity wasn’t just hard. It was economically penalized by the structure of digital advertising.

 

At the same time, Florida passed Senate Bill 266 (SB 266), effectively banning DEI initiatives in public universities. In one of the most diverse states in the country, it became risky—even forbidden in some settings—to talk explicitly about race, structural inequity, or bias in the exact way our data demanded.

 

The paradox was clear:

- The laws made it harder to name the problem.
- The data made it impossible to ignore.

 

In the midst of that, the state did something else: through the First Lady’s office, Florida awarded a Casey DeSantis Breast Cancer Research grant that helped catalyze MammoChat, the platform I would later spin out. Florida would proudly fund AI for breast cancer while restricting the language we could use to describe who was dying and why.

 

While I was trying to architect a more equitable future, my father was living the endgame of the old one.

 

As his disease progressed, he became too sick and complex for home. We moved him to a nursing home. My mother, with her Alzheimer’s, would sometimes forget he was ill and get furious when he couldn’t help in the ways he used to.

 

At one point, his care team needed to interrogate his pacemaker and asked me, the physician–scientist son, if I knew the serial number of the device in his chest.

 

I laughed, instinctively. It’s the last thing you want to do when your loved one is fragile and the care team is serious. “How on earth would I know that?” I thought. “Isn’t that your job? Isn’t it in your system?”

 

The laugh died quickly.

 

Because I realized something in that moment:

I had access to SQL—the query language for databases—dumps of hospital databases with millions of coded events. I could query cancer registries and national repositories. I could write a grant to analyze hundreds of thousands of radiology studies.

 

And yet, as a son, I could not easily and reliably find one pacemaker’s serial number for the man who birthed me.

 

The system that never forgets a CPT code had somehow made the precise identity of a device it had implanted into my father’s heart practically unknowable at the bedside.

 

If that’s what it looks like for someone with two doctorates and a full understanding of the system, imagine what it looks like for everyone else.



## Alzheimer’s, Culture, and COVID

 

My father’s decline played out over years. In those final seasons before COVID, he lived in the nursing home during the week and came home on weekends. We tried to make it feel like a rhythm, not a sentence.

 

My mother’s Alzheimer’s edited their shared life down to one sharp fragment:

her father’s old warning—don’t marry a creole; he will leave you.

 

She could no longer remember forty-plus years of marriage.

She could no longer remember the nights they worked side by side in the hospital.

She could no longer remember migrating in their fifties so their sons could have a better life.

 

When Sunday came and we had to take him back to the nursing home, she would get furious. Not a little irritated—betrayed. In her mind, the prophecy had come true. The creole man was leaving her, just as her father said he would.

 

Alzheimer’s had stolen the evidence of his faithfulness.

All that remained was the bias.

 

I will never forget that. Very few things move me to my core now. That did. The way disease stripped away the story of a marriage and left only the prejudice. The way my father endured it with patience, never resenting her, always returning the next weekend anyway.

 

He kept coming home, week after week, to a woman who no longer remembered the life they had built. He remembered for both of them.

 

Then COVID came, and the doors closed.

 

Lockdowns went into effect. Nursing homes shut themselves off from the world to protect their residents. After that, he never came home again.

 

He died there, in that nursing home, in the shadow of a pandemic that turned too many lives into statistics. Somewhere, he is part of some spreadsheet row for mortality in men with comorbid hypertension, diabetes, kidney failure. To the system, he is a data point. To me, he was a universal man—the embodiment of all the tangled histories that made me.



## A Universal Future

 

Now I am the father.

 

I have three teenage daughters from my first marriage and two young sons and a baby girl on the way from my second. Their mother—my wife—is Spanish Moroccan. She speaks Spanish and Arabic more easily than English. She is Muslim. She is from Spain, not Latin America, and she has told me what makes healthcare feel safe, respectful, and culturally competent.

 

She has not had her first mammogram yet. In our kitchen, she has told me she wants a system that speaks her language—literally and metaphorically—and treats her not as an outlier but as part of the fabric.


 

That is not an accident. It is the direct continuation of what my father meant when he told us we came from a long line of universal men. He took the raw material of colonialism, racism, and poverty and built a philosophy of inclusion out of it. He reconciled with the father who left his mother. He claimed his siblings. He married the Indian girl from Gasparillo his in-laws would have forbidden. He raised Dougla sons in a house full of differences and called it blessing, not burden.

 

Marrying a Spanish Moroccan woman is not me rebelling against my past.

It is me honoring it.

 

I am my father’s son.

A universal man raising universal children.

And I will not dare disappoint him.

 

My mother now lives with me. She is 88, physically strong, mentally elsewhere. She will die with me, not in an institution. It is the least I can do for the girl from Gasparillo who became Pearl in England and Surajee again in the quiet of our kitchen when she forgets which name she is supposed to answer to.



## Dividends & Deaths

 

As I stood in June 2025 on a small boat off Englishman’s Bay, holding the urn that contained my father’s ashes, all of this was in the air.

 

The bay lay below us, the same perfect curve it had been when he was a barefoot boy in Charlotteville. The hills were still thick with trees. The same land his grandfather’s line had once controlled was now the backdrop for returning him to the sea.

 

On the boat with me were all my children, my three older teenage girls, my two young boys, a Seventh-day Adventist pastor, the captain and my pregnant wife, our daughter still in utero, turning quietly in the dark. The pastor prayed. I read the eulogy I had written for my father’s funeral. Then we tipped the urn, and the ashes of a universal man fell into water that belongs to no one and everyone.

 

In that moment, the lines converged:

- Granny, the Black seamstress who sent her son away by boat so he wouldn’t be trapped behind God’s back.
- Mama, the Hindu matriarch whose yard grew sugar cane, mangoes, pawpaw, guava, and governor plums, and whose daughter broke every rule by marrying a creole.
- Grandma and Grandpa, the Venezuelan-Trinidadian step-grandparents who represented a line that once owned 900 acres and never owned responsibility for what had been done with it.
- My parents, who migrated in their fifties so their sons could go to schools they’d only ever read about.
- Henrietta Lacks, the mother of precision medicine, whose cells made other people rich and whose descendants had to hire a civil rights attorney to get anyone to admit what had been taken.
- My patients, whose data I had turned into models and grants and companies.
- My own children, who will inherit whatever world I help build from here.

 

I have spent my career inside the machinery that turns illness into data and data into money. I helped build a precision medicine company at CHOP. I trained at Stanford while ontologies and ontic billing codes were being wired into the core of medicine. At UCSF, I wrote NIH grants to mine open datasets and deployed deep learning to predict disease years before diagnosis. In Florida, I tried to use AI to fix breast cancer disparities in a state that banned DEI, even as my own father and mother were being slowly undone by chronic diseases and a system that profits from failure.

 

What I have learned is simple and devastating:

 

In modern healthcare, dividends rise and deaths fall.

The profits go up.

The bodies go down.

 

Henrietta’s dividends went to companies. Her death stayed with her family.

My father’s illnesses generated bills. His death is a line item in a nursing home ledger.

My mother’s Alzheimer’s generates codes and appointment slots. Her death will not move a stock.

 

We call it precision medicine when we tailor a drug to a mutation. But the precision is often applied to the people most easily measured—those whose genomes already sit in freezers, whose bills are easily coded, whose lives fit the architecture of the system.

 

The rest—the Black woman shut out of trials, the Latina who can’t take off work for a study visit, the immigrant whose records are fragmented across states and systems—remain imprecisely managed and imprecisely mourned.

 

This book, Dividends & Deaths, is my attempt to tell that truth without flinching and to lay out a path away from it.

 

It is part memoir, part indictment, part blueprint.

 

It was written for Henrietta.

For my parents, Leonard and Surajee (Pearl).

For my children, who deserve a healthcare system that does not profit from failing them.

For your family, whether you know it or not, already wired into someone’s model.

 

I did not stop being a doctor.

I changed what I think a doctor’s job should be.

 

In the chapters that follow, we will move from:

- the Billable Code as the core unit of value,
- to the genomic consortia that scaled on Eurocentric data,
- to the AI models that inherit those biases,
- to the legal frameworks that already exist to protect patients but are rarely used,
- and finally to a new architecture—an Open Provenance Token System and an equitable governance layer—designed to return some of the dividends of this economy to the people whose lives make it possible.

 

Henrietta Lacks is still in the freezer.

My father is in Englishman’s Bay.

My mother is in my living room, now having sporadic unintelligible conversations with herself and others throughout the day as her mental status fluctuates. 

My children are upstairs, scrolling on devices that will soon be triaging their health.

 

The mother of precision medicine never got to see what her cells would do.

The least we can do now is stop pretending this economy is neutral.

 

Dividends rise.

Deaths fall.

It is time to rewrite who pays—and who gets paid—when your body enters the system.
